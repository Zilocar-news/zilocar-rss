Airbag-Inflator Recalls Didn't End With the Takata Wave — They Moved to the Side Curtains (NHTSA Data)

Airbag-inflator rupture recalls are still being filed, but they are not the recalls people remember. Of the 20 filed on US cars and light trucks since January 2023, 15 cover a side-curtain or roof-rail inflator — against 2 of the 87 filed during the 2014-2017 Takata frontal wave. Only 2 of the 20 name Takata at all.

Key facts

  • 20 airbag-inflator rupture recall campaigns filed on US cars and light trucks from 2023-01-01 to 2026-11-19, covering 722,304 vehicles as filed.
  • 15 of those 20 (75.0%) cover a side-curtain or roof-rail inflator. In the 2014-2017 wave it was 2 of 87 (2.3%).
  • 75 of the 87 wave campaigns covered frontal inflators — the steering-wheel and dashboard bags. Since 2023 only 5 of 20 do.
  • The mechanism flipped too: 50 of 87 wave campaigns describe propellant degradation; 10 of 20 recent ones describe a weld joint failing or an end cap detaching, and only 2 describe propellant degradation.
  • Only 2 of the 20 recent campaigns name Takata in their text — but so do only 4 of the 87 wave campaigns, so the word is a poor test and we make no supplier claim.
  • The largest recent campaign, Toyota's 26V748000, was filed 2026-11-12: 314,029 vehicles, model years 2002-2017, Takata front passenger inflators. That is 82.8% of all 2026 units from one filing.
  • Scale, stated honestly: the wave covered 42,048,309 vehicles. The recent stream covers 722,304 — about 1.7% of it.

Are airbag inflator recalls still happening?

Yes — 20 of them on cars and light trucks since the start of 2023, seven in 2026 alone. The public story of airbag inflators ended around 2017, when the Takata recalls became the largest automotive recall in US history and then faded from the news. NHTSA's own Part 573 filings did not stop.

The volume collapsed, which is why it stopped being news. It did not go to zero.

What changed between the Takata wave and now?

The part of the car being recalled moved, from the bags in front of you to the ones above your head. This is the finding, and it is the one thing in this analysis that no news coverage states.

A frontal inflator sits in the steering wheel or the passenger dashboard. A side-curtain or roof-rail inflator sits above the headliner and fires the long bag that drops down over the side windows. They are different parts, made by different suppliers, and they are failing for different reasons.

Is it the same defect as Takata?

No — the campaigns describe a different failure, in their own words. Takata's problem was chemical and time-dependent: ammonium-nitrate propellant degrading after years of heat and humidity, so the inflator over-pressurises and ruptures. Fifty of the 87 wave campaigns say exactly that.

The recent side-inflator campaigns describe manufacturing faults instead:

  • Weld joint failure — 5 campaigns. BMW's wording: the inflator "may fail at the weld joint."
  • End cap detachment — 5 campaigns. General Motors' wording: the roof-rail "inflator end cap may detach from the inflator or the inflator sidewall may rupture."
  • Unspecified manufacturing defect — 5 campaigns, all Chrysler side-curtain filings.
  • Propellant degradation — 2 campaigns only, and both are the genuine Takata stragglers.

That distinction matters to a used-car buyer for one practical reason. A degradation defect gets worse with age and exposure, which is why Takata campaigns kept expanding to older cars in hot states. A weld or end-cap defect is built in at the factory and does not spread to cars outside the affected build range.

Which cars are in the recent recalls?

Eight of the 20 campaigns cover a Chevrolet or GMC, and most of those are Silverado and Sierra roof-rail inflators from the 2018-2019 model years. The concentration is real but mild — nothing like the Takata wave, when a single supplier's inflators put almost every major brand into the same recall.

The rest spread across Ram, Dodge, Chrysler, BMW, Buick, Cadillac, Nissan, Hyundai, Toyota and Lexus. Every campaign, with its model years and unit count, is in the frozen dataset.

How old are the cars being recalled?

Old enough that the registered owner is rarely the person the recall letter was written for. In 9 of the 19 recent campaigns that list model years, the oldest model year covered is at least ten years before the filing date. The median gap is nine years. The two extremes are the Takata stragglers:

  • 25V748000, BMW, filed 2025-10-31: 2000-2001 X5, a Takata PSDI-4 inflator in a sport steering wheel. Twenty-five model years.
  • 26V748000, Toyota, filed 2026-11-12: 2002-2010 Lexus SC430, 2003-2008 Corolla, 2005-2006 Tundra, 2005-2007 Sequoia, 2011 IS250, 2016-2017 GX460 and 4Runner. Twenty-four model years at the oldest.

This is the part that bears on buying a used car. Owner-notification letters go to the address on the registration. A 2003 Corolla recalled in late 2026 has plausibly had four or five owners, and the letter reaches whoever the state has on file — not the person about to buy it on a forecourt. The recall stays open and free to fix for the life of the vehicle; nobody is required to tell the next buyer it exists.

Our own analysis of NHTSA completion data found the same gap from the other direction: is it safe to buy a used car with an open recall shows the biggest recalls are the least likely to be fixed.

What a VIN check can and cannot tell you here

It can tell you whether a recall is open on that specific car today. It cannot tell you whether a past one was really repaired. Being precise about this matters more than usual on an inflator recall, because the consequence of an unrepaired one is severe.

A Zilocar VIN check can show:

  • whether open recalls exist on the vehicle, and how many
  • accident and damage records, including airbag-deployment records — relevant because a replacement airbag assembly is itself sometimes the recalled part
  • odometer readings and rollback indications
  • salvage and junk auction records, theft records, ownership history and sales-listing history

It cannot show:

  • whether a recall repair was performed, or performed correctly — see does a completed recall mean a used car is fixed
  • remedy status, firmware or flashed state on a specific unit
  • an open NHTSA investigation, which is not a recall — see what does recall closed mean on a vin check
  • the legal title brand, which is NMVTIS and your state DMV

For repair status on an inflator specifically, run the VIN through NHTSA's free recall lookup and then ask a franchised dealer to read the vehicle's campaign history. For context on the earlier generation of these campaigns, see how to check if a used car has an open airbag recall.

Method

Source. NHTSA's Part 573 recall flat file, https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/ffdd/rcl/FLAT_RCL_POST_2010.zip, retrieved 2026-11-19: HTTP 200, 14,993,527 bytes, Last-Modified: Thu, 19 Nov 2026 08:04:33 GMT, and ZipFile.testzip() returned None, so the archive is intact rather than a truncated download. At retrieval the file held 248,434 rows and 15,417 distinct campaigns.

Freshness, computed on the same basis as the figures. max(RCDATE) across all record types is 20261116, but that is a single equipment campaign (26E081000). Restricted to RCLTYPECD='V' the frontier is 20261112, which is the basis every number here uses. Quoting the unrestricted frontier would overstate how current a vehicle-recall study is by several days.

Universe N. Campaigns are kept when COMPDESC contains AIR BAG and either the campaign text names Takata or the component/text contains INFLATOR and the text contains RUPTURE. That yields 166 campaigns across all record types.

Scope restriction. RCLTYPECD='V' means vehicle, not car — it carries motorcycles, buses, heavy trucks, motorhomes and trailers. Campaigns are kept only when the Part 573 filer (MFGTXT) appears on an explicit allow-list of 51 named car and light-truck manufacturers, published in full in the methodology file beside the CSV so the boundary can be disputed rather than guessed at. A name regex was deliberately not used. This takes 166 campaigns to 132; of the 34 excluded, 27 are the inflator supplier's own equipment filings (Takata Corporation, TK Global, Key Safety Systems/Joyson, Autoliv) and the rest are Daimler Trucks, Spartan Motors and Forest River.

One matching trap worth stating, because it decides the headline. MFGTXT is not canonical. The same company appears as General Motors, LLC and General Motors LLC; as Chrysler (FCA US, LLC) and Chrysler (FCA US LLC); as Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC and Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC. with a trailing period. Matching is therefore done on a normalised key — lowercased, & to and, non-alphanumerics collapsed. An exact string match drops real car campaigns silently, including this study's largest, 26V748000, whose filer string is Toyota Motor Engineering & Manufacturing.

Classification and aggregation. Each campaign's inflator position is read from NHTSA's own COMPDESC label plus campaign text: frontal where COMPDESC contains FRONTAL; side-curtain/roof-rail where it contains SIDE/WINDOW or the text contains CURTAIN, ROOF RAIL or ROOF-RAIL; unspecified otherwise. One row per campaign, not per make/model/year line. Units are POTAFF as filed. Eras compared: 2014-01-01 to 2017-12-31 and 2023-01-01 to 2026-11-19.

Reversal check, and its result. This is a composition claim, so the check most likely to overturn it is the treatment of the position-unspecified campaigns. The wave has 10 of 87; the recent era has 0 of 20. Assigning every unspecified wave campaign to side-curtain — the most adverse assumption available — moves the wave's side share from 2.3% to 13.8%, against 75.0% since 2023. The direction survives the worst case. All 10 of those campaigns are 2014-2016 Honda, Subaru, Chrysler, Ford, Nissan and Toyota filings, i.e. the classic frontal Takata recalls, so counting them as side-inflator campaigns is deliberately perverse.

Independent verification. 26V748000, 25V748000, 24V198000 and 26V325000 were re-checked against api.nhtsa.gov/recalls/campaignNumber and matched the flat file exactly on filer, component, report-received date and units affected.

Known limits.

  1. POTAFF is the manufacturer's launch-time estimate of units potentially affected. It is not a count of cars still on the road, still unrepaired, or still in the US, and it cannot support any claim about how many dangerous cars are out there.
  2. The file does not reliably identify the inflator supplier. Only 2 of the 20 recent campaigns name Takata, but only 4 of the 87 wave campaigns do either — so the absence of the word is not evidence, and no supplier-level claim is made here. Joyson and Autoliv are named only in their own equipment filings, which this vehicle-campaign basis excludes.
  3. 2026 is an incomplete year and NHTSA's published record lags filings by several days.
  4. The recent stream is lumpy: 82.8% of 2026 units come from one campaign. Single-year unit totals are not a trend.
  5. Position is read from NHTSA's labels and prose, not from engineering records.
