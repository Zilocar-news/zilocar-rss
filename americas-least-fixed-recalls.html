America's Least-Fixed Recalls: The Mega-Recalls Leaving Millions of Cars Driving With Open Safety Defects

TL;DR: A Zilocar analysis of 10,367 completion-tracked U.S. vehicle recalls (NHTSA dataset 6axg-epim, data as of July 22, 2026) finds an estimated 149.5 million recalled vehicles still unrepaired, roughly one in three. The single worst mega-recall, Ford's October 2025 rearview-camera campaign covering 1.45 million vehicles, is just 0.70% fixed. Ninety-six large recalls since 2020 sit under 50% completion.

Key findings

  1. Ford's 1.45-million-vehicle rearview-camera recall (25V695000, Oct 2025) is 0.70% fixed — about 1.44 million cars still driving with the defect.
  2. Ford has an estimated 19.9 million vehicles still unrepaired from recalls since 2020 — more than GM (5.7M), Honda (5.2M) and Toyota (5.1M) combined.
  3. 96 mega-recalls since 2020 (100k-plus vehicles each) sit under 50% completion, covering 49.1 million vehicles.
  4. Across all 10,367 completion-tracked recalls, an estimated 149.5 million vehicles remain unrepaired; overall units-weighted completion is just 66.2%.
  5. Honda's Takata driver-side air-bag recall (20V026000) is still only 7.91% fixed six years after the 2020 remedy — about 1.32M of 1.44M inflators.
  6. Safety-critical recalls are fixed no faster than convenience ones: 65.1% vs 66.4% — and air bags alone sit at 61.1% across 109.4M vehicles.
  7. Ford's 4.38-million-vehicle F-150 trailer-brake recall (26V104000, Feb 2026) is 16.12% fixed — about 3.68 million trucks, the biggest single pool of unfixed recent vehicles.

By the numbers

  • 0.70% fixed — Ford's worst single mega-recall (25V695000, 1,448,655 vehicles), about 1.44 million still unrepaired. The lowest completion rate of any recent 1M-plus recall in the data.
  • 49,104,236 vehicles — the pool sitting across 96 recalls (since 2020, 100k-plus each) that are less than half-fixed.
  • ~149.5 million vehicles — the estimated total still unrepaired across all 10,367 completion-tracked recalls, at 66.2% overall units-weighted completion.

Every figure below is from a Zilocar analysis of NHTSA "Recalls Data" (Socrata dataset 6axg-epim), data as of July 22, 2026.

In the middle of the loudest recall year the U.S. auto industry has ever recorded, the number nobody is publishing is the one buyers actually care about: not how many cars got recalled, but how many are still broken.

Here is one answer. In October 2025, Ford recalled 1,448,655 vehicles because the rearview camera image could fail to display. Nine months later, that recall is 0.70% fixed. That is roughly ten thousand cars repaired out of nearly one and a half million. About 1.44 million are still on the road with the defect NHTSA flagged.

It is the most extreme mega-recall outlier in NHTSA's live completion data, and it is not alone. When you rank recalls not by how big they are but by how few have been fixed, a national backlog comes into focus: an estimated 149.5 million recalled vehicles that were never repaired.

Finding 1: One Ford recall is 0.70% fixed

Ford's 25V695000 is the sharpest example of a pattern that runs through the recent data: enormous recalls, tiny fix rates. It is verified to the unit against NHTSA's own recalls API (which, notably, carries no completion figure — that data lives only in dataset 6axg-epim).

It is not even Ford's only sub-4% camera recall. A separate September 2025 campaign (25V572000) covers another 1,456,417 vehicles at 3.47% fixed. Together, two Ford rearview-camera recalls account for roughly 2.8 million vehicles almost entirely unrepaired.

Finding 2: Ford has more unrepaired cars than GM, Honda and Toyota combined

The record-recall story of 2025-2026 has been about Ford's sheer volume. The completion data adds the second half of the sentence: those recalls are also the least completed.

Since 2020, Ford issued 398 recalls covering 42.6 million vehicles at just 53.2% units-weighted completion — the lowest fix rate of any major automaker in the data. That leaves an estimated 19.9 million Ford vehicles unrepaired, more than General Motors, Honda and Toyota combined.

Finding 3: Nearly 50 million vehicles are less than half-fixed

Zoom out from any one brand and the scale of the backlog is a national figure. Since 2020, 96 recalls of 100,000 vehicles or more sit under 50% completion, together covering 49,104,236 vehicles — a pool of nearly 50 million cars that are less than half repaired. That count was verified against a server-side aggregate of the same dataset.

Finding 4: The never-fixed classics stay stuck for years

Recency explains the low fix rates in Table 2. It cannot explain Table 1. Here the filter is strict: only recalls of 500,000-plus vehicles where the remedy has been available two or more years. These are cars whose owners have had ample time — and the fix rates are still dismal.

Honda's Takata driver-side air-bag recall (20V026000) is 7.91% fixed six years after its 2020 remedy: about 1.32 million of 1.44 million inflators that can rupture are still installed. Ford's Explorer A-pillar trim recall (24V031000, 1.89 million vehicles) sits at 17.10%.

Finding 5: Life-or-death recalls aren't fixed any faster

You might assume the scariest recalls get prioritized. They don't. Bucketing all 10,367 recalls by defect type, safety-critical recalls (air bags, steering, brakes, fuel and fire, powertrain, suspension, tires, seat belts) are fixed at 65.1% units-weighted completion — slightly slower than convenience and electronics recalls at 66.4%.

Finding 6: 2026's biggest recall is 16% fixed

The largest live recall of the year is Ford's February 2026 F-150 campaign (26V104000): 4,381,878 trucks whose trailer lighting and brakes may not function. At 16.12% fixed, roughly 3.68 million pickups still carry the defect — the single biggest pool of unfixed recent vehicles on the road. It is early days for that recall, so the number should climb; but at this size, even a fast ramp leaves millions of trucks waiting.

Methodology

Source: NHTSA "Recalls Data," Socrata dataset 6axg-epim on data.transportation.gov (no auth). On 2026-07-22 I pulled a bulk CSV of all vehicle-type recalls carrying a populated completion rate via $where=recall_type='Vehicle' AND completion_rate IS NOT NULL ($limit=50000, no pagination needed): N=10,367 recalls, report-received dates 2011-05-25 to 2026-06-15 (completion rates are populated by NHTSA quarterly/annual reports from 2015-Q1 onward). Cleaning per the plan's caveats: exactly 3 artifact rows carried completion_rate above 100% (Bugatti 133%, Proterra 3,380% and 108%, all on 3-140 units) and were capped at 100%; zero rows had potentially_affected <= 0. Per row I computed estimated_unfixed = round(potentially_affected * (1 - min(completion_rate,100)/100)); potentially_affected is the manufacturer's at-filing population estimate. Table 1 filters to potentially_affected>=500,000 and report_received_date<=2024-06-30 (remedy available 2+ years) to dodge the recency confound; Table 2 to report_received_date>=2024-01-01; Table 3 aggregates report_received_date>=2020-01-01 with completion<100, subsidiary names normalized to parent brand; Table 4 buckets the NHTSA component field. I verified the 7 headline recalls' affected counts and subject text against the independent api.nhtsa.gov/recalls/campaignNumber API (all matched to the unit) and cross-checked scorecard (a) and Ford's Table-3 totals against server-side SoQL aggregates (96 recalls / 49,104,236 vehicles and 398 recalls / 42,611,333 vehicles — both matched exactly).

What the numbers can and cannot tell you:

  • "Estimated unfixed" is directional, not a headcount. It is affected population multiplied by (1 − % fixed). "Affected" is the manufacturer's at-filing estimate, and "% fixed" is a snapshot from NHTSA's latest quarterly/annual reporting — not a VIN-confirmed live count of cars still needing repair.
  • Completion coverage starts at 2015-Q1. Recalls before that, and any recall NHTSA has not reported a rate for, carry no completion figure and are excluded. N=10,367 is the completion-tracked universe, not every recall ever issued.
  • Recent recalls look worse partly because they are recent. Owner notification and parts supply lag months, so Table 2's low percentages partly reflect timing. Table 1 removes this by requiring the remedy to have been available two-plus years.
  • The very lowest outliers are inflated by scrapped cars and unreachable owners. Mazda 15V674000 (0.62%) covers 1989-1998 vehicles and Keystone 17V755000 (2.15%) covers RVs. Ford 25V695000 (0.70%, MY2015-2019) is the strongest recent "less than 1% fixed" example. Mazda technically edges Ford at the very bottom; it is reported honestly in Table 1, but the recent, reachable-owner Ford recall is used as the headline outlier.
  • Brand grouping and component buckets carry residual error. Table 3 normalizes subsidiary names to parent brands (a server-side LIKE check matched Ford's total exactly); residual mis-grouping is possible for smaller brands. Table 4 uses NHTSA's component field, and 4,711 recalls fell into "other/unclassified" and are shown separately rather than forced into a bin.
  • Completion could not be triangulated against a second source. The api.nhtsa.gov recalls API used for belt-and-suspenders verification carries no completion rate; fix-rate figures exist only in dataset 6axg-epim.

What this means if you're buying used

A national completion rate is not a verdict on any one car. But it does reset the odds. With an estimated one in three recalled vehicles never repaired, a used car you are considering is more likely than not to carry recall history worth checking — and the older, higher-mileage cars that dominate the used market are exactly the ones caught in these long-tail recalls.

The honest limits matter here. A completion percentage is a national snapshot, not a per-vehicle status, and the only way to know a specific car is to check its VIN. A Zilocar VIN check surfaces whether a recall is present or outstanding on a given vehicle (recall presence and count — not the remedy or firmware status), alongside accident and airbag-deployment records, odometer and rollback discrepancies, salvage and junk auction records, theft records, ownership count and past sales-listing history. It does not confirm that a recall was actually performed — for that, run the VIN through NHTSA's free recall lookup and have a dealer verify the remedy before you buy. Used together, they turn a scary national statistic into a specific, answerable question about the car in front of you.

FAQ

How many recalled cars in the U.S. are still unfixed? Across 10,367 completion-tracked vehicle recalls in NHTSA's data, a Zilocar analysis estimates roughly 149.5 million recalled vehicles remain unrepaired, because overall units-weighted completion is 66.2%. That is a directional figure, not a VIN-confirmed live count. Data as of July 22, 2026.

What is the least-fixed car recall in America right now? Among recalls of 1 million-plus vehicles, Ford's October 2025 rearview-camera recall (25V695000) covers 1,448,655 vehicles and is only 0.70% fixed. An older Mazda ignition-switch recall (15V674000) is technically lower at 0.62%, but it covers 1989-1998 vehicles, many long scrapped.

Which automaker has the most unrepaired recalled vehicles? Ford. On recalls since 2020, an estimated 19.9 million Ford vehicles remain unrepaired, more than GM, Honda and Toyota combined. Ford issued 398 recalls covering 42.6 million vehicles at 53.2% units-weighted completion.

Do dangerous safety recalls get fixed faster than minor ones? No. Safety-critical recalls sit at 65.1% completion versus 66.4% for convenience and electronics recalls — essentially the same, slightly slower. Air bag recalls alone are 61.1% complete across 109.4 million vehicles.

Does a low national completion rate mean a specific car still has an open recall? Not necessarily. Completion rate is a national snapshot from manufacturer reporting, not a per-vehicle status. Check the VIN — a VIN check surfaces whether a recall is present or outstanding, and NHTSA's free lookup plus a dealer can confirm the remedy.

Why are some recent recalls at such low completion rates? Partly because they are recent: owner notification and parts supply lag months. Table 1 removes this by only including recalls where the remedy has been available two or more years.
