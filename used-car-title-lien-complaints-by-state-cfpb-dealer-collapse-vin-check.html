Which States Lead in Used-Car Title and Lien-Payoff Complaints (CFPB Data) — and How to Check for an Unpaid Lien Before You Buy

Federal complaints about never receiving a car title after a paid-off loan have more than doubled since 2024, and they cluster in a handful of states. From 7,954 CFPB "Vehicle loan or lease" title complaints, Georgia leads per resident at 3.96 per 100,000 — the highest rate of any state with a stable sample — while California and Texas top raw counts but rank below average. A VIN check flags a churned or salvage car; confirming a clean title and cleared lien takes NMVTIS and your state DMV.

Which states have the most car-title and lien-payoff complaints?

By population-adjusted rate, Georgia leads the nation among states with a statistically stable base: 3.96 title complaints per 100,000 residents, from 448 raw filings. New Jersey (3.85), Maryland (3.73), Nevada (3.63), Florida (3.12) and Virginia (3.12) follow. These are the states where "I paid but never got my title" reaches a federal regulator most often, relative to how many people live there.

Raw counts tell a different, population-driven story, which is exactly why we lead with the per-capita cut. California files the most title complaints in absolute terms (844), then Florida (732) and Texas (661) — but those three are among the four most populous states. Normalize by July-1-2025 Census population and the picture reverses: California drops to 2.14 per 100,000 (rank 20), Texas to 2.08 (rank 21) and New York to 1.97 (rank 27), all below the national rate. The states that lead on both raw volume and per-capita rate — Georgia, New Jersey, Maryland and Florida — are the durable finding. This is the same reshuffle we found ranking used car loan complaints by state, where dividing by population moved the obvious suspects.

By the numbers: how fast is this growing, and across which lenders?

Title complaints are not a stable background rate — they are climbing steeply. In the narrow title tier, apples-to-apples January–October counts ran 783 in 2024, 994 in 2025 and 1,770 in 2026: up 126% in two years and 78% year over year. (We cap November 2026 as an incomplete month — 46 filings through the 16th against a 2026 run rate near 200 a month — so it is excluded from the trend.) The single core sub-issue, "Unable to receive car title after payoff," shows the same shape: 911 in 2024, 1,215 in 2025, 1,779 so far in 2026.

Crucially, this is a market-wide failure, not one bad actor. The most-complained-about company accounts for just 9.6% of all-time title complaints, with Ally (700), Santander (501), Westlake (457), Wells Fargo (369), Capital One (329) and Chase (308) spread across the rest; in 2026 the mix tilts toward subprime-heavy servicers (Santander 272, Westlake 182, Ally 170). Tricolor Holdings — the collapsed subprime dealer driving the headlines — has zero rows in this database. The per-state counts measure the broad "buyer pays, lien is never cleared, title never transfers" failure across all lenders, not Tricolor-specific filings.

Why this is in the news: dealer collapses that strand the title

The data describes a failure mode that a string of 2025–2026 dealer and lender collapses has made concrete — an ongoing consumer-protection theme, not a single breaking event. Subprime "buy-here-pay-here" lender Tricolor Holdings placed more than 1,000 employees on leave around September 6, 2025 and filed bankruptcy about September 10, reported as a Chapter 7 liquidation. The Chapter 7 trustee, Anne Elizabeth Burns, told the court in October 2025 that the evidence pointed to fraud of "extraordinary proportion," with founder and CEO Daniel Chu "firmly at the center."

The legal track has two separate milestones that are easy to conflate. A DOJ criminal indictment was unsealed in the Southern District of New York on December 17, 2025 (US v. Chu, 25-cr-579), charging conspiracy, bank fraud and securities and wire fraud; former CFO Jerome Kollar and executive Ameryn Seibold pleaded guilty the day before and are cooperating, while Chu pleaded not guilty. A separate SEC civil complaint (SEC v. Chu, 26-cv-7041, Litigation Release LR-26612) was filed later, on August 18, 2026, alleging Tricolor raised more than $1.9 billion through asset-backed securitizations by double-pledging the same auto-loan receivables, leaving an estimated collateral gap around $800 million. The criminal case is from December 2025; the SEC case is from August 2026.

For a buyer, the mechanism matters more than the indictment. When a dealer finances its lot through a floor-plan lender, that lender holds the titles until each car sells. "Selling out of trust" means the dealer pockets your payment but never repays the floor-plan lender, so the lien is never released and the title cannot transfer. In Tucson, Metro Auto Center closed in 2025 and its owners filed personal Chapter 7; floor-plan lender Manheim Automotive Financial Services alleged 46 vehicles were sold out of trust for more than $360,000 in damages, Arizona's MVD logged roughly 50 title complaints, and the dealer's surety bond was only $25,000 against them. Near Baton Rouge, Geaux Autoplex in Zachary, Louisiana closed around September 2025 over floor-plan trouble (no bankruptcy filing was confirmed), and the Louisiana Used Motor Vehicle Commission counted 15 vehicles with the same unresolved title problem. If you are buying from a financially shaky dealer, our guide to how to verify a used car title is clean before buying and the breakdown of what happens to your car loan when the dealer goes bankrupt cover the paperwork and the payments.

How do I check for an unpaid lien before buying — and what does a VIN check actually prove?

Confirming a clean title and a cleared lien takes two authoritative checks, and a VIN-history report is neither of them. Run a NMVTIS report (vehiclehistory.bja.ojp.gov, operated under the U.S. DOJ's Bureau of Justice Assistance) for the legal title brand — salvage, junk or flood — plus odometer and total-loss history. Then run a state DMV title and lien search by VIN at the DMV that issued the title: the DMV record is the only place an active lienholder appears, because NMVTIS does not carry lien data. For New York buyers, our walkthrough of the NYS title check shows exactly what a DMV lien lookup returns.

A VIN-history report, including a Zilocar check, answers a different question: it vets the car's past, not its financing. Use it to catch the warning signs that often travel with a shaky or churned vehicle — salvage and junk-auction records, odometer rollbacks, accident and airbag-deployment history, theft records, prior owners, and especially sales-listing history (a car bounced between dealers or relisted repeatedly is a red flag). What it cannot do is confirm the legal title brand, show whether a prior loan was actually paid off, or reveal an active lien. For a fuller pre-purchase routine when buying off a dealer lot, see how to check a used car's history before buying from a dealer.

What a VIN check can and can't tell you here

A VIN check and this complaint study answer different questions, and neither proves a clean title on its own. The CFPB analysis is a research-desk map of where buyers complain about stranded titles; it is not tied to any one VIN. A VIN check vets the car, and its real value in a dealer-collapse scenario is early warning: ownership and sales-listing churn, salvage-auction records, odometer rollbacks, accident and theft history, and open-recall presence. Those signals can tell you a car is risky before you hand over money.

What a VIN check cannot do, and what this article will never imply it can: it does not show the legal title-brand classification (it shows salvage and junk-auction records, which is not the same as the state's title brand), it does not show an active lien or whether a loan was paid off, and it does not confirm whether an open recall was remedied. For the title brand, cede to NMVTIS; for the lien, cede to the state DMV; for recall remedy, cede to NHTSA or the franchised dealer.

A Zilocar VIN check screens for open-recall presence and surfaces accident and airbag-deployment, salvage and junk-auction, odometer, theft, ownership and sales-listing history, specs, safety ratings and valuation — the vetting that matters most on subprime or churned inventory. Use it to spot the warning signs, then confirm the title at NMVTIS and the lien at your DMV before you pay.

Frequently asked questions

How do I check if a used car has an unpaid lien before I buy it? Run a title and lien search at the state DMV that issued the car's title, using the VIN. The DMV title record is the only source that shows an active lienholder. A VIN history report does not carry lien data; a NMVTIS report shows title brand and salvage history but not active liens either.

Can a VIN-history report tell me whether a car's loan or lien is paid off? No. Loan balances, lienholders and payoff status are lender and DMV records, not vehicle-history data, and are in no VIN feed. A VIN check surfaces accidents, salvage and junk-auction records, odometer, theft, ownership and sales-listing history and open-recall presence — not financing.

What is the difference between a title brand and an active lien, and which does a VIN check show? A title brand (salvage, junk, flood) is a permanent state classification of the car's condition, confirmed through NMVTIS. An active lien is a lender's legal claim on the title, held in the state DMV record. A VIN check shows neither as a legal fact — it shows salvage-auction records, not the title brand, and no lien data at all.

If a dealership goes bankrupt after I buy, do I still have to make my car payments? Yes, if you financed through a third-party lender. Your loan contract is with that lender, not the dealer, so missing payments damages your credit and risks repossession. The dealer's collapse can strand your title, but it does not cancel your loan. Keep paying and pursue the title through the DMV and the dealer's surety bond.

What is "selling out of trust" and how does it leave a buyer with no title? Dealers finance their inventory through a floor-plan lender that holds the titles until each car sells. Selling out of trust means the dealer took your money but never paid that lender, so the lien is never released and the title cannot transfer to you. In Tucson's Metro Auto case, the floor-plan lender alleged 46 vehicles were sold out of trust.

Which states have the most complaints about not getting a car title after payoff? By raw count, California (844), Florida (732) and Texas (661) lead, but that tracks population. Per 100,000 residents, Georgia (3.96) leads among states with a stable sample, followed by New Jersey, Maryland, Nevada, Florida and Virginia. California, Texas and New York fall below the national rate once normalized.

I paid off my car loan but never received the title — what are my steps? First ask the lender for a lien release in writing, then take it and your VIN to the state DMV to apply for a clear title. If a dealer closed without delivering it, file a complaint with your state's dealer board and claim against the dealer's surety bond — though bonds are small ($25,000 in Arizona's case against roughly 50 claims).

How long should it take to receive a clear title after buying or paying off a car? Timelines vary by state, but most give a dealer a delivery window measured in weeks to hand over the title, and lenders usually send a lien release within a few weeks of payoff. If weeks pass with nothing, treat it as a warning sign and escalate to the DMV and your state's dealer board before more time goes by.

Known limits.

  • DC (5.77 per 100k, n=40) and Delaware (4.06, n=43) post higher rates than Georgia on small samples. They are flagged low-base, not crowned, and they appear at ranks 1 and 2 in the frozen CSV's own title_rank_per_100k column. Georgia is described throughout as the highest-rate state among those with a stable base (n≥100), never as the outright national leader.
  • 110 of the 7,954 title-tier records carry no state and are excluded from the per-state math.
  • The dealer-collapse peg is a narrative explanation of the failure mechanism, not a data trace. Tricolor Holdings has zero complaints in the vehicle-loan database. The per-state counts measure the broad title/lien failure mode across all lenders.
  • A complaint count measures propensity to complain to a federal regulator — shaped by consumer awareness, financial sophistication and local legal-aid presence — not a verified rate of dealer or lender wrongdoing.
  • The database refreshes, so a later re-pull will differ slightly. The frozen CSV linked in Sources is the exact version these numbers come from.
