Is Now a Good Time to Buy a Used Car in 2026? What the Freshest Manheim Data Actually Shows

No, the used-car cooldown is not over, and the auction market is not roaring back. The freshest full-month Manheim Used Vehicle Value Index (July 2026 data, published in early August) fell about 1.4% month-over-month to roughly 210, on normal seasonal depreciation, and sits only about +1.3% year-over-year. Retail listing prices remain elevated near a June record of about $27,000. Beware a stale reading of 124.3 circulating online: it is roughly a decade old.

This is a deliberate follow-up to our July read that wholesale prices were cooling. That read was correct, and the newest data confirms it rather than reversing it.

Key facts

  • Freshest release: Manheim Used Vehicle Value Index for July 2026 (full month), published around August 7, 2026. There is no August full-month release yet; August data publishes in early September.
  • July 2026 index: approximately 210, down about 1.4% month-over-month, up about +1.3% year-over-year (seasonally, mix- and mileage-adjusted). The exact full-month decimal carries minor uncertainty; the direction (down MoM, roughly +1-2% YoY) is well cross-verified.
  • Mid-July 2026 (first 15 days): 211.5, down 0.6% versus June, up 2% year-over-year (Cox Automotive).
  • June 2026 (full): 212.9, up 0.1% month-over-month (essentially flat), up 2.1% year-over-year.
  • Direction since the March 2026 peak: normalizing and softening, not three straight monthly gains.
  • Average used-vehicle listing price: about $27,027 in June 2026, up roughly 6% year-over-year, the highest since summer 2023 (Cox Automotive).
  • Inventory: about 2.14 million used units, roughly 47 days' supply at the end of June 2026; sub-$15,000 cars were tightest at about 33 days' supply.
  • Debunked figure: a "124.3 index, +2.1% YoY, third consecutive monthly increase" comes from an archived circa-2014 Manheim release on a non-dated URL. It is not 2026 data.

Are used-car prices going up or down right now?

Down slightly, on a month-over-month basis. The newest full-month Manheim Used Vehicle Value Index, covering July 2026 data, came in near 210, about 1.4% below June, driven by ordinary seasonal depreciation. Year-over-year it is up only about 1.3%. The mid-July release from Cox Automotive told the same story: 211.5, down 0.6% from June, up 2% from a year earlier.

Both the mid-month and full-month readings point the same direction: down. The wholesale (auction) market is easing, not firming. Any headline claiming wholesale prices "jumped" or posted a "third straight monthly rise" in August does not match Cox Automotive's own releases.

Did wholesale prices really post a third straight monthly increase?

No. There was no third consecutive monthly increase. The verified 2026 monthly path shows a peak in March, a roughly 1% easing through April and May, an essentially flat June (up 0.1%), a down mid-July, and a down full July. That is a normalizing market drifting off a spring high, not a three-month rally.

Where did the "124.3 index" and the "jumped in August" claims come from?

They trace to a stale source. A generic, non-dated Manheim press-release page currently serves an archived release from around August 2014, whose body text references Federal Reserve rate hikes that had not yet begun and "2013 and 2014 levels." That release reported an index of 124.3 and +2.1% year-over-year.

In 2026 the Manheim index sits in the low 210s; it peaked near 235 in early 2022. A reading of 124.3 is impossible for 2026. The coincidence that the stale 2014 figure was +2.1% year-over-year, the same as the real June 2026 figure, is exactly what makes the error easy to repeat. Treat any non-dated Manheim index URL, and any 2026 headline built on 124.3 or a "third straight rise," as unreliable.

What is the average used-car price in 2026, and how tight is cheap inventory?

Cox Automotive's average used-vehicle listing price was about $27,027 in June 2026, up roughly 6% year-over-year and the highest since summer 2023. That is a retail listing figure, not the same as a transaction price. A separate Edmunds metric, the average transaction price for a 3-year-old vehicle, was $31,548 in Q1 2026. These are different measures and should not be blended into a single "average."

Inventory told a two-tier story at the end of June 2026: about 2.14 million used units, roughly 47 days' supply market-wide, but sub-$15,000 cars were far tighter at about 33 days' supply. Affordable units sell fastest and hold value best, which is the accurate version of the idea that cheaper cars move quickest.

So is now a good time to buy, or should I keep waiting?

There is no sharp wholesale-driven discount on the way. Auction values are easing seasonally, not collapsing, and retail listing prices sit near record highs. Wholesale generally leads retail, so a modest auction dip in July does not translate into a quick sticker-price cut; retail follows with a lag and is still elevated. The gains from waiting for the market are marginal.

That shifts the leverage from timing to the individual car. In a flat-to-softening market, the difference between a good deal and a bad one is whether a specific vehicle's asking price is fair, whether it has been cut or quietly marked up, and how long it has been sitting. That is a per-car question, and it is answerable.
