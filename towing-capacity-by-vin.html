Towing Capacity by VIN: Why a VIN Alone Won't Give You a Tow Rating

A VIN alone will not give you a towing capacity. A VIN decode returns the build descriptors — year, make, model, engine, drivetrain, assembly plant, body class and a GVWR weight-class range — but no tow-rating field. Checked live on 2026-11-03, NHTSA's free vPIC decoder returned 142 variables for a sample F-150 VIN with only 46 populated, and no towing-capacity field among them. The real number comes from the manufacturer's calculator or guide, the door label and owner's manual, or the build sheet.

Towing capacity by VIN: the key facts

  • The VIN carries no tow rating. A VIN decode gives the build — make, model, year, engine, drivetrain, plant, body class — and GVWR only as a weight-class range, never the towing capacity, GCWR, maximum trailer weight or tongue weight.
  • vPIC proves it. A live decode of sample F-150 VIN 1FTFW1E57MFA00000 on 2026-11-03 returned 142 variables with only 46 populated, including "Class 2F: 7,001-8,000 lb" for GVWR; every trailer-named field returned Not Applicable or empty.
  • The rating follows the build, not the badge. The tow/trailer package, axle (final-drive) ratio, cab-and-bed combination and drivetrain decide it, so two identically-badged trucks can tow different maximums.
  • Three authoritative sources set or return it: the manufacturer's VIN- or build-keyed calculator/guide, the door label plus owner's manual, and the build sheet/RPO list.
  • No history report returns a tow rating — not Carfax, AutoCheck, Bumper or any paid VIN report. They report the truck's past, not its capacity.

Can the VIN number tell me the towing capacity?

No. A VIN decode identifies the truck's build but does not carry its tow rating, because the rating is not encoded in the 17 characters. This is a verified result, not an assumption: on 2026-11-03 a live NHTSA vPIC API decode of a sample Ford F-150 VIN returned 46 populated fields — Make FORD, Model F-150, Model Year 2021, Series F-Series, Engine 5.0L, Drive Type 4WD, Plant Dearborn, Body Class Pickup, and "Gross Vehicle Weight Rating From: Class 2F: 7,001-8,000 lb." GVWR comes back only as a weight class range, not a single number. The four trailer-named fields (Trailer Type Connection, Trailer Body Type, Trailer Length, Other Trailer Info) all returned Not Applicable or None. There was no GCWR, no maximum trailer weight and no tongue weight.

Why do two trucks with the same year, make, model and engine have different tow ratings?

Because the rating is set by the as-built configuration, not the badge on the tailgate. The tow/trailer package, the axle (final-drive) ratio, the cab-and-bed combination and the drivetrain each move the number, so two trucks that decode to the same year, make, model and engine can carry different maximums when those options differ. The VIN decodes the build descriptors; the manufacturer — not the VIN — maps a specific equipped configuration to a pound figure. The axle ratio is the single biggest lever, which is why the build record matters. Published pound figures for specific trucks vary widely by configuration, so this page does not quote a specific truck's maximum — the authoritative figure is the one the OEM calculator returns for your exact build.

1. The door label and the owner's manual — the as-built reference

Start at the truck. Under FMVSS No. 110, every vehicle rated 10,000 lb GVWR or less carries a Tire and Loading Information label; Ford states the payload on it is "the maximum payload for your vehicle as built by the assembly plant." NHTSA confirms federal rules require GVWR and GAWR on every vehicle, usually on the driver's door or door jamb (the Certification label). The nuance for towing: the tow rating itself — maximum trailer weight and GCWR — is generally not printed on these labels; it lives in the owner's manual or towing guide. The exception is GM trucks (Chevrolet/GMC), which add a door-jamb Trailering Information label reflecting the factory configuration. Reading these descriptors off the truck is the same discipline as reading a window sticker by vin — the label states what the plant installed.

2. The manufacturer's VIN- or build-keyed calculator — the authoritative number

This is the only source that maps a configuration to pounds, and all three big truck makers publish one free. Ford offers an online Towing Calculator for 2020-and-newer F-Series: you sign in or enter the VIN, add occupant, cargo and accessory weights, and it returns Max Tongue Load and Max Trailer Weight (assuming 10% tongue load for conventional, 15% for fifth-wheel/gooseneck). Ford also publishes the 2026 Ford RV and Trailer Towing Guide as a PDF. GM hosts a downloadable Trailering Guide and a Load Calculator in the myGMC app, and Chevrolet publishes a Silverado trailering/payload page. Ram publishes model-year Payload & Towing Guides; a previously available Ram VIN towing lookup is reported to have been discontinued or limited to older model years, so Ram's current authoritative tool is the build/trim-keyed guide rather than a live VIN calculator.

3. The build sheet / RPO option list — for the axle ratio

The axle ratio and the presence of a tow package are read from the build data. GM lists them as RPO (Regular Production Option) codes; Ford and Ram carry them on the window sticker and build record. Because the axle ratio is the biggest lever on the rating, confirming it is how you tell whether a truck is actually equipped to tow what its trim implies — the same option-decoding you would do to check what features and options a used car has by VIN.

What's the difference between towing capacity, GCWR, GVWR and payload?

These four limits are distinct and often confused. GVWR (Gross Vehicle Weight Rating) is the most the loaded truck itself may weigh — what vPIC returns as a class range. GAWR (Gross Axle Weight Rating) is the limit for each axle. Payload is what you can put in the truck (people plus cargo plus tongue weight), and the as-built figure is on the door label. Towing capacity (maximum trailer weight) and GCWR (Gross Combined Weight Rating, truck plus trailer) are the trailer limits — the two numbers the VIN does not carry and the OEM calculator does.

What a VIN check can and can't tell you here

A VIN check cannot produce a tow rating. NHTSA's free vPIC decoder cannot (verified above), and no paid vehicle-history report does either, because the number is not in the VIN. For the tow figure, the manufacturer's calculator or guide is the authoritative free source — cede the number to it.

For a work truck that history is the point: towing-adjacent collision or structural damage, a salvage/junk-auction entry, or an odometer gap matters more than a catalog maximum — a rating assumes a sound truck. Be clear about the limits. A Zilocar check screens for recall presence the way NHTSA's free tool does, but it does not confirm a recall remedy or flash, report NHTSA investigations (PE/EA), show per-unit dealer firmware status, or show the legal title brand — it shows the junk/salvage auction and ownership records that precede a brand. NMVTIS and the state DMV are the title-brand authorities.

Who should use which

  • You want the tow number for a specific truck: the OEM calculator or towing guide, free — Ford's VIN-keyed calculator for a 2020+ F-Series, GM's Trailering Guide and myGMC Load Calculator, Ram's model-year Payload & Towing Guide.
  • You are standing at the truck: read the door Tire and Loading label and Certification label for payload and GVWR/GAWR, the owner's manual for the tow figure, and (on a GM truck) the door-jamb Trailering label.
  • You want to confirm the axle ratio or tow package: the window sticker, build sheet or RPO option list.
  • **You are deciding whether to buy this used truck:** a VIN history check, to see accident/structural damage, salvage or junk-auction records, odometer gaps and ownership before you trust it with a trailer.

What to verify by VIN before you pay

  1. Accident and structural damage — especially rear-end and frame-adjacent damage on a truck you intend to tow with.
  2. Salvage or junk auction records — a truck that passed through one is a different purchase than its trim suggests.
  3. Odometer / rollback — a gap changes both the value and the wear on a towing drivetrain.
  4. Airbag-deployment status — a marker of a serious prior impact.
  5. Recall presence — screen for open recall presence, then confirm remedy with a dealer using the VIN (NHTSA, not a history report, holds remedy status).
  6. The axle ratio and tow package — from the window sticker or build sheet, so the truck is equipped to tow what the trim implies.
