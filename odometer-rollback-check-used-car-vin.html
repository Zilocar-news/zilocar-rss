Georgia Just Made Odometer Fraud a Felony — Here's How to Spot a Rolled-Back VIN Before You Buy

Direct answer: To check if a used car's odometer was rolled back, pull a VIN vehicle-history report and compare the mileage on every dated record — title transfers, service and inspection entries, and past sales listings. If a later reading is lower than an earlier one, or barely moves across years, that is the classic rollback pattern. A VIN check can surface those discrepancies, but it cannot certify true mileage or guarantee no tampering. Odometer fraud is already a federal felony; Georgia's Senate Bill 293 made it a state felony as well, effective July 1, 2026.

Update — 2026-07-31: Georgia SB 293's felony odometer-fraud penalties took effect July 1, 2026 as scheduled — no delay or amendment. Tampering that Georgia once prosecuted only as a misdemeanor is now a state felony carrying reported fines of $1,500–$10,000 plus possible jail time, alongside the two new state investigators and added used-car-broker oversight the bill created.

Key facts

  • Law: Georgia Senate Bill 293 (2025–2026 session) elevates odometer tampering and fraud from a state misdemeanor to a felony.
  • Effective date: July 1, 2026 (now in effect).
  • What it covers: knowingly altering an odometer reading; knowingly falsifying the odometer reading on a certificate of title; false statements on a title application.
  • Reported criminal penalty: fines of $1,500–$10,000 plus possible jail time. It also adds two state investigators and more oversight of used-car brokers.
  • Civil remedy (Georgia AG guidance): treble damages — three times actual damages — or a statutory floor, whichever is greater.
  • Federal baseline: odometer tampering was already a federal felony under 49 U.S.C. Chapter 327; SB 293 closed the gap where Georgia prosecuted "clocking" only as a misdemeanor.
  • National scale (NHTSA estimates): 450,000+ vehicles sold each year with false odometer readings; $1 billion+ in annual consumer cost. Carfax estimates ~2.5 million vehicles on US roads have tampered odometers.
  • Credited catalyst: lawmakers credited an Atlanta News First (ANF) Investigates odometer series; sponsor Rep. John Corbett said the issue "was not on the committee's radar" until he saw the reports.

What did Georgia's new odometer law actually change?

Georgia Senate Bill 293 makes it a felony, effective July 1, 2026, to knowingly alter an odometer reading or to knowingly falsify the odometer reading recorded on a certificate of title. Before this, Georgia prosecuted odometer "clocking" only as a state misdemeanor, even though the same conduct was already a federal felony under 49 U.S.C. Chapter 327. The law also adds two state investigators and greater oversight of used-car brokers.

The bill's path was unusual. SB 293 began as a measure to expand free license plates for disabled veterans, passed the Georgia Senate 56-0 in March 2025, then sat in the House Motor Vehicles Committee for over a year. It emerged in March 2026 as a broad Title 40 rewrite covering dealer plates, temporary operating permits, and certificate-of-title penalties, with Rep. John Corbett (R-Lake Park) attaching the odometer-felony fix rather than filing it standalone. Because the vehicle carried several unrelated provisions, the reporting is best read as lawmakers crediting an ANF investigation for putting the issue on the agenda — not as proof the coverage alone caused passage.

Isn't odometer rollback an old-technology problem?

No. Odometer rollback is not a paper-odometer relic. Modern digital and electronic odometers are rewritten with inexpensive plug-in tools, which is exactly why a 2026 felony statute and cross-record mileage checks still matter. NHTSA estimates more than 450,000 vehicles are sold each year in the US with false odometer readings, at a cost exceeding $1 billion annually, and Carfax has estimated roughly 2.5 million vehicles on the road carry tampered odometers.

The ANF investigation that lawmakers credited illustrates the scale. First-time buyer Shadaja Johnson bought a 2013 Honda Ridgeline advertised on Facebook Marketplace at about 153,000 miles; vehicle-history and emissions records showed the true reading was near 285,000 — a gap of roughly 132,000 miles. In a spot check of nine Facebook Marketplace cars against history reports, ANF reported that five (some summaries say six) had major mileage discrepancies, with the largest gap cited at up to 170,000 miles. Another buyer, John Groneman, was reported to have a Lexus with a 77,500-mile discrepancy.

(Note: ANF's July 22, 2026 follow-up about a nearly blind, disabled buyer and a 2013 Mini Cooper concerns predatory dealer sales practices, not odometer rollback. It is part of the same used-car franchise but is not a mileage-fraud case.)

What does odometer rollback look like in a vehicle history report?

Rollback shows up as a mileage number that moves the wrong way — or barely moves — across a car's dated records. Because odometers cannot legally decrease, any later reading below an earlier one is the single clearest red flag. The table below maps the signals a buyer or history report can catch.

Signal in the reportWhat it means
A later record shows FEWER miles than an earlier recordClassic rollback — mileage cannot decrease
Mileage flat or barely moving across years of recordsOdometer likely disconnected or frozen
Emissions/inspection or service reading far above the listingListing mileage understated
A prior sales-listing mileage higher than the current listingRolled back between listings
"Not Actual Mileage" or "Exceeds Mechanical Limits" title brandFederally flagged mileage discrepancy

How do you check if a used car's odometer was rolled back?

Start with the paper trail and the records that outlive it. Compare the seller's advertised mileage against the vehicle's odometer disclosure statement on the title, then against independent readings captured at emissions tests, state inspections, and service visits. A VIN vehicle-history report pulls those readings together so you can see whether the number ever dropped or stalled — the exact pattern that would have exposed the ANF cases, where a car listed at 153k had earlier records near 285k.

Use the authoritative free tools first. NHTSA's odometer-fraud consumer pages explain how to read a disclosure statement and how to report suspected fraud, and NHTSA advises pairing any records check with an independent mechanic's inspection. A commercial VIN history report — such as a Zilocar VIN check — is a useful companion here: it runs an odometer/rollback screen across successive title, service, and sales-listing records, and, as a differentiator, shows past and current sales-listing history with the mileage on each listing, so you can catch a VIN previously advertised at higher miles than the current ad claims.

What a VIN check can and can't tell you here

A VIN mileage-history check is a first-line screen, not a certificate. Read honestly, it is powerful for catching the discrepancies that fraud investigations rely on — and clearly bounded on what it cannot prove.

A rollback performed before the vehicle ever generated a mileage record, or between reported records, can leave no discrepancy to catch — which is precisely why NHTSA also advises an independent mechanic's inspection and comparing the odometer disclosure statement. The VIN check is prevention: it has no role in the new Georgia felony statute or the post-sale civil remedy. The law punishes the seller after the fact; the history check helps you walk away first.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if a used car's odometer was rolled back before I buy it? Pull a VIN vehicle-history report and read the mileage on each dated record. If a later title, service, inspection, or sales-listing reading is lower than an earlier one, or the number stalls implausibly across years, that gap is the classic rollback signal. Pair it with an independent mechanic's inspection.

Is odometer fraud a felony? It is already a federal felony under 49 U.S.C. Chapter 327. Georgia's SB 293 elevated state-level tampering from a misdemeanor to a felony, and that change took effect July 1, 2026, with reported fines of $1,500–$10,000 plus possible jail time. Other states vary; check your own statute.

Can a digital or electronic odometer be rolled back? Yes. Modern digital and electronic odometers are rewritten with cheap plug-in tools, so rollback is not a paper-odometer relic. That is a core reason a 2026 felony statute and cross-record VIN mileage checks still matter.

Does a VIN check guarantee the mileage is accurate? No. It surfaces mileage readings across recorded events and flags drops or stalls, but it cannot certify true mileage or prove a car is untampered. A rollback done before any record existed, or between reports, may leave no discrepancy to catch.

How common is odometer fraud in the US? NHTSA estimates more than 450,000 vehicles are sold each year with false readings, costing consumers over $1 billion annually. Carfax has estimated about 2.5 million vehicles on US roads carry tampered odometers.

What should I do if I already bought a car with a rolled-back odometer? Report it to NHTSA's Office of Odometer Fraud Investigation and your state attorney general's consumer-protection division. Georgia's AG cites treble (triple) damages or a statutory floor, whichever is greater. Keep your title, disclosure statement, and listing records.

Does the new Georgia felony law get my money back? The felony statute punishes the seller. Georgia offers a separate civil remedy (treble damages). Neither replaces prevention — a VIN mileage-history check and an independent inspection help you avoid the purchase in the first place.
