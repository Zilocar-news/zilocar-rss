Georgia Just Made Odometer Fraud a Felony — Here's How to Spot a Rolled-Back VIN Before You Buy

Direct answer: To check a used car's real mileage before buying, pull a VIN vehicle-history report and compare the mileage on every dated record — title transfers, service and inspection entries, and past sales listings. If a later reading is lower than an earlier one, or barely moves across years, that is the classic rollback pattern. A VIN check can surface those discrepancies, but it cannot certify true mileage or guarantee no tampering. Odometer fraud is already a federal felony; Georgia's Senate Bill 293 made it a state felony too, effective July 1, 2026.

Update — 2026-08-28: Georgia SB 293's felony odometer-fraud penalties have now been in force since July 1, 2026. Atlanta News First's "Uncovered" series carried the case that put the issue on the legislature's agenda again on August 16, 2026 — a first-time buyer's 2013 Honda Ridgeline advertised at about 153,000 miles whose history records showed far more. ANF's original investigation and its July 7, 2026 follow-up both put the truck's true reading near 285,000 miles — a gap of roughly 132,000. One caveat we could not reconcile: ANF's own February 3, 2026 story on the broker's arrest gives 232,000 miles instead, an ~79,000-mile gap. Treat 285,000 as the reported figure and ~79,000 miles as the undisputed floor of the rollback.

Key facts

  • Law: Georgia Senate Bill 293 (2025–2026 session) elevates odometer tampering and fraud from a state misdemeanor to a felony.
  • Effective date: July 1, 2026 (now in effect).
  • What it covers: knowingly altering an odometer reading; knowingly falsifying the odometer reading on a certificate of title; false statements on a title application.
  • Criminal penalty: a felony. Atlanta News First reports fines of $1,500 to $10,000 and possible jail time; the specific felony class and prison term are not consistently stated across coverage.
  • Civil remedy (Georgia AG guidance): three times actual damages or $10,000, whichever is greater, plus attorney's fees.
  • Extra provisions: two new state investigators and greater oversight of used-car brokers.
  • Federal baseline: odometer tampering was already a federal felony under 49 U.S.C. Chapter 327; SB 293 closed the gap where Georgia prosecuted "clocking" only as a misdemeanor.
  • National scale (NHTSA estimates, 2002 study): 450,000+ vehicles sold each year with false odometer readings; $1 billion+ in annual consumer cost. Carfax estimates ~2.5 million vehicles on US roads carry tampered odometers.
  • Credited catalyst: lawmakers credited an Atlanta News First (ANF) Investigates odometer series; sponsor Rep. John Corbett said the issue "was not on the committee's radar" until he saw the reports.

What did Georgia's new odometer law actually change?

Georgia Senate Bill 293 makes it a felony, effective July 1, 2026, to knowingly alter an odometer reading or to knowingly falsify the odometer reading recorded on a certificate of title. Before this, Georgia prosecuted odometer "clocking" only as a state misdemeanor, even though the same conduct was already a federal felony under 49 U.S.C. Chapter 327. The law also funds two new state investigators and adds oversight of used-car brokers.

The bill's path was unusual. SB 293 began as a measure to expand free license plates for disabled veterans, passed the Georgia Senate 56-0 in March 2025, then sat in the House Motor Vehicles Committee for about a year through two recommittals. In the 2026 session it was rewritten into a broad motor-vehicle-code package, with committee chair Rep. John Corbett (R-Lake Park) attaching the odometer-felony fix rather than filing it standalone. Because the vehicle carried several unrelated provisions, the reporting is best read as lawmakers crediting an ANF investigation for putting the issue on the agenda — not as proof the coverage alone caused passage. Gov. Brian Kemp signed the bill into law; the exact signature date is not pinned in the available sources.

Isn't odometer rollback an old-technology problem?

No. Odometer rollback is not a paper-odometer relic. Modern digital and electronic instrument clusters are rewritten in minutes with inexpensive plug-in OBD-II "correction" tools, and fraudsters use out-of-state title hops — title washing — to break the mileage paper trail. Private and broker sales on platforms like Facebook Marketplace also bypass the disclosure discipline of franchised dealers, which is exactly why a 2026 felony statute and cross-record mileage checks still matter. NHTSA's long-cited estimate is that more than 450,000 vehicles are sold each year in the US with false odometer readings, at a cost exceeding $1 billion annually (from a 2002 NHTSA study, DOT HS 809 441 — dated, but still the standard figures), and Carfax has estimated roughly 2.5 million vehicles on the road carry tampered odometers.

The ANF case that lawmakers credited shows how this lands on a buyer. First-time buyer Shadayja Johnson, 25, and her mother Latrice paid $6,400 for a 2013 Honda Ridgeline advertised on Facebook Marketplace at about 153,000 miles, sold by broker Dennis Lewis (DeDe Auto Brokers, in Gwinnett County). A Carfax report showed a far higher true reading — near 285,000 miles, a gap of roughly 132,000, with the dealership that originally sold the truck having serviced it regularly right up to its auction sale at that mileage. (ANF's February 3, 2026 story on the arrest instead gives 232,000 miles, an ~79,000-mile gap; we cite the higher figure because both the original investigation and the July 2026 follow-up use it, and flag the discrepancy rather than hide it.) The family caught it on day two: a check-engine light sent them to a mechanic, who found two faulty catalytic converters and flagged the mileage discrepancy. They demanded and received a refund. Lewis turned himself in to Gwinnett County police on February 3, 2026 on charges including odometer fraud, false advertising, and failure to produce business records — misdemeanor charges under the old law, since SB 293 is not retroactive to his conduct. If you are buying from a broker or private seller, the same discipline used to spot the fraud applies to any listing; it overlaps with how to check a used car's history before buying from a dealer.

What does odometer rollback look like in a vehicle history report?

Rollback shows up as a mileage number that moves the wrong way — or barely moves — across a car's dated records. Because odometers cannot legally decrease, any later reading below an earlier one is the single clearest red flag. The table below maps the signals a buyer or history report can catch.

Signal in the reportWhat it means
A later record shows FEWER miles than an earlier recordClassic rollback — mileage cannot decrease
Mileage flat or barely moving across years of recordsOdometer likely disconnected or frozen
Emissions/inspection or service reading far above the listingListing mileage understated
A prior sales-listing mileage higher than the current listingRolled back between listings
"Not Actual Mileage" or "Exceeds Mechanical Limits" title brandFederally flagged mileage discrepancy

How do you check a used car's real mileage before buying?

Start with the paper trail and the records that outlive it. Compare the seller's advertised mileage against the vehicle's odometer disclosure statement on the title, then against independent readings captured at emissions tests, state inspections, and service visits. A VIN vehicle-history report pulls those readings together so you can see whether the number ever dropped or stalled — the exact pattern that exposed the ANF Ridgeline, where a truck listed at ~153k had earlier records near ~285k. Because wear tells its own story, weigh the displayed number against the tires, pedals, seat bolstering, and steering wheel, and against what typical use looks like at that age — our breakdown of at what mileage cars start having problems is a useful yardstick for whether a "low-mileage" claim is plausible. A suspiciously low reading on a worn, older truck is the same alarm covered in what to check before buying an older high mileage used car.

Use the authoritative free tools first. NHTSA's odometer-fraud consumer pages explain how to read a disclosure statement and how to report suspected fraud, and NHTSA advises pairing any records check with an independent mechanic's inspection. A commercial VIN history report — such as a Zilocar VIN check — is a useful companion here: it runs an odometer/rollback screen across successive title, service, and sales-listing records, and, as a differentiator, shows past and current sales-listing history with the mileage on each listing, so you can catch a VIN previously advertised at higher miles than the current ad claims. That listing-history dimension is what would have flagged a Marketplace ad understating a truck's miles by roughly 130,000.

What a VIN check can and can't tell you here

A VIN mileage-history check is a first-line screen, not a certificate. Read honestly, it is powerful for catching the discrepancies that fraud investigations rely on — and clearly bounded on what it cannot prove.

Surface junk/salvage auction records, ownership history, accident/damage, theft (NICB), recall presenceAdjudicate a "Not Actual Mileage" brand, act as NMVTIS, or catch a rollback that left no record

A rollback performed before the vehicle ever generated a mileage record, or in the gap between reported records, can leave no discrepancy to catch — which is precisely why NHTSA also advises an independent mechanic's inspection and comparing the odometer disclosure statement, and why a title-history/NMVTIS check belongs alongside it. The VIN check is prevention: it has no role in the new Georgia felony statute or the post-sale civil remedy. The law punishes the seller after the fact; the history check helps you walk away first, and it is a screening signal — not legal proof of mileage or evidence for a fraud claim.

Frequently asked questions

How can I check a used car's real mileage before I buy it? Pull a VIN vehicle-history report and read the mileage on every dated record — title transfers, service and inspection entries, and past sales listings. If a later reading is lower than an earlier one, or barely moves across years, that gap is the classic rollback signal. Confirm with an independent mechanic's inspection and the title's odometer disclosure statement.

Is odometer fraud a felony in Georgia now, and what changed on July 1, 2026? Yes. Georgia Senate Bill 293 took effect July 1, 2026, elevating odometer tampering from a state misdemeanor to a state felony. The conduct was already a federal felony under 49 U.S.C. Chapter 327; SB 293 closed Georgia's gap and added two state investigators plus more used-car-broker oversight.

What are the penalties for odometer fraud in Georgia — fine, jail, or being sued? Two separate tracks. Criminally, SB 293 makes tampering a felony; Atlanta News First reports fines of $1,500 to $10,000 and possible jail time, though the felony class and prison term are not stated consistently across coverage. Civilly, and separately, a Georgia victim can sue for three times actual damages or $10,000, whichever is greater, plus attorney's fees.

Can a digital or electronic odometer be rolled back? Yes. Modern digital and electronic instrument clusters are rewritten in minutes with cheap plug-in OBD-II tools, so rollback is not a mechanical-odometer relic. Fraudsters also use out-of-state title hops to break the mileage paper trail, which is why cross-record VIN mileage checks still matter.

Does a VIN check guarantee the mileage is accurate? No. It surfaces mileage readings across recorded events and flags drops or stalls, but it cannot certify true mileage or prove a car is untampered. A rollback done before any record existed, or in the gap between reports, may leave no discrepancy to catch.

How common is odometer fraud in the US? NHTSA's cited estimate is more than 450,000 vehicles sold each year with false readings, costing consumers over $1 billion annually — figures from a 2002 NHTSA study that remain the standard numbers. Carfax has estimated about 2.5 million vehicles on US roads carry tampered odometers.

How do I check mileage on a car I found on Facebook Marketplace or from a broker? Treat the advertised number as unverified. Get the VIN before you visit, run a mileage-history report on it, and compare the ad's mileage against every earlier title, inspection, and listing record. Private and broker sales skip the disclosure discipline of franchised dealers, so a records check plus a mechanic matters most there.

What should I do if I already bought a car with a rolled-back odometer? Report it to NHTSA's Office of Odometer Fraud Investigation and your state attorney general's consumer-protection division. In Georgia, the AG cites a civil remedy of three times actual damages or $10,000, whichever is greater, plus attorney's fees. Keep your title, odometer disclosure statement, and listing records.
