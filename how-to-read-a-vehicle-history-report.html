What Does a Vehicle History Report Actually Show? How to Read Every Section (and What It Leaves Out)

A vehicle history report is an aggregated record of a car's past, keyed to its 17-character VIN: title status and brands, reported accidents and damage, odometer readings, ownership changes, some service events, open recalls, and theft flags. It shows what someone reported to a participating database — not everything that happened to the car. It can flag a recorded salvage auction or a rollback; it cannot prove the legal title brand, confirm a recall was fixed, or capture a cash repair no one logged.

Key facts

  • Two authorities sit under every report. NMVTIS (vehiclehistory.gov, run by the U.S. DOJ) is the federal source for title, brand, odometer, total-loss and salvage data. NHTSA (nhtsa.gov/recalls) is the free source for open safety recalls. Commercial reports layer accident, service and listing data on top.
  • NMVTIS carries exactly five indicators: current title status and last title date, brand history, odometer reading, total-loss history, and salvage history.
  • NMVTIS is the only U.S. system where insurers, auto recyclers, junk yards and salvage yards must report by federal law — salvage/junk operators at least every 30 days, insurers' total-loss declarations generally within 30 days.
  • NHTSA's VIN tool shows open, unrepaired recalls for at least the past 15 calendar years — presence only, not whether a given owner completed the repair.
  • "Reconditioned" is a dealer refurbishment label, not a title brand, and by itself does not mean prior major damage.
  • Carfax states it draws from over 100,000 sources. Outside NMVTIS-mandated title/salvage/insurer data, most reporting is voluntary — records that never enter a participating database never appear.
  • Commonly missing from every report: cash and out-of-network accident repairs, work by non-reporting independent shops, unclaimed collisions, unreported minor damage, and private-party maintenance.

Where do vehicle history reports get their data?

Vehicle history reports aggregate records from many separate networks, and each network only knows what it was told. The consistent categories are: state DMVs and motor-vehicle agencies (title brands, registrations, ownership, odometer readings); insurance carriers (claims and total-loss declarations); auto auctions (sale history, announced structural damage, condition notes); franchise dealerships and some large service chains (service and repair records); police, fire and law enforcement (accident and theft reports); salvage and junk yards; and fleet, rental and government agencies. Carfax states it draws from over 100,000 sources.

The distinction that matters most is legal versus voluntary. Under federal law, insurers, auto recyclers, junk yards and salvage yards must report to NMVTIS regularly — that data is close to complete. Everything else, including most service and much accident data, arrives by voluntary participation, so a record that never enters a participating database never appears — which is why two providers can show two different pictures of the same VIN.

What does "reconditioned" mean on a Carfax report — is it bad?

"Vehicle Reconditioned" means a dealer performed reconditioning or restoration work to bring the car to resale-ready condition, and reported that work. It ranges from routine maintenance to cosmetic or mechanical repair. It is reported by the dealer, it is not a title brand, and by itself it does not indicate prior major damage. Treat it as a service note, not a warning.

Major damage lives in a different place entirely — the title-brand and salvage sections. If a car was declared a total loss and rebuilt, that shows as a salvage or rebuilt status, not as "reconditioned." Confusing the two is a common misread that either scares buyers off a clean car or lulls them past a branded one.

What is a title brand, and does a report prove it?

A title brand is a descriptive label a state motor-vehicle agency applies to a title — such as salvage, rebuilt, junk, flood, or lemon — and NMVTIS is the authoritative federal source for it. Brand terminology and the thresholds that trigger it are set by each state and vary, so there is no single national definition; NMVTIS notes that a brand like "flood" or "rebuilt" is retained even when a car re-titles across state lines. For the legal brand, the authorities are NMVTIS (vehiclehistory.gov) and the issuing state's DMV.

A commercial report surfacing a "salvage auction" line is not the same as the state-issued brand. In Zilocar's sample report, the Junk & Salvage section shows one illustrative record from IAA (Insurance Auto Auctions), dated 01/14/2025, reported with a salvage title and a rebuilt title issued 06/12/2026 — useful signals, but the legal classification is what the state stamps, so confirm the current brand at NMVTIS and the DMV before you decide. What does an odometer "rollback" or "possible rollback" flag mean?

An odometer section compares successive mileage readings collected at title transfers, registrations, auctions and service visits. A "possible odometer rollback" or mileage-sequence flag means the readings are missing or out of order — a later reading lower than an earlier one, or a suspicious gap. It is a prompt to investigate, not a proof of fraud, and it says nothing certain about the true mileage between two recorded readings.

Read the summary and the timeline together, because they can disagree. In the sample report the odometer summary says "No odometer brands, mileage-sequence issues, or auction odometer brands reported," while the detailed timeline still flags a "possible odometer rollback" at the final entry — exactly the conflict to resolve before buying, since the detail rows carry specifics the summary rounds off.

Does a vehicle history report show every accident?

No. The accidents-and-damage section carries collisions that reached a participating database — most often through an insurance claim, a police report, or an auction disclosure. Every report of this kind carries a version of the same disclaimer: damage reporting is not comprehensive, and not all accidents, repairs, or structural issues are reported to databases. A blank section is not proof the car was never in a crash.

When a record does exist, read it in full: the sample report shows one accident with a location (left rear), type (rear-end plus mechanical), date (02/2025), condition ("run & drive"), and airbag status ("intact"). Airbag-deployment status is the single most useful field, because a deployment implies a significant impact and expensive restraint-system work. Cash repairs and out-of-network body work are the classic omissions — the same gap how to check if a used car has flood or salvage history exposes in cars that were "title-washed" clean.

Does a report tell me if a recall was actually fixed?

No — this is the most misread section of any report. The recall area mirrors NHTSA's free VIN lookup, which returns open (unrepaired) safety recalls for at least the past 15 calendar years. A recall stays "open" until an authorized dealer performs the free repair, at which point it drops off. So the report shows recall presence, not remedy: it does not confirm a specific owner completed the fix, does not cover recalls older than about 15 years, and does not show a VIN the manufacturer hasn't yet populated.

It also does not track NHTSA investigations (the PE and EA stages) or technical service bulletins — those are separate from formal recalls. To confirm the fix was done, the dealer's service record for that VIN is the authority, not any history report. It's worth understanding what does recall closed mean on a vin check, because "no open recalls" and "recall completed for this car" are not the same claim.

What is NMVTIS, and how is it different from Carfax or AutoCheck?

NMVTIS (the National Motor Vehicle Title Information System, at vehiclehistory.gov) is the federal title-fraud database run by the U.S. Department of Justice. It carries exactly five indicators — title status and last title date, brand history, odometer reading, total-loss history, and salvage history — and it is the only U.S. system to which insurers, recyclers, junk yards and salvage yards must report by law. It is the authoritative source for the legal title, brand, odometer and salvage record.

Crucially, NMVTIS deliberately excludes the rest. In its own words, private providers offer beneficial information "not intended to be included in NMVTIS, such as vehicle repair histories, recall information, and other care and maintenance data." So commercial reports and VIN tools are not competing with NMVTIS on title data — they add accident, service, listing, recall-screening and valuation layers on top of it. Use NMVTIS for the brand, a commercial or VIN report for the history around it.

Why is my car's service history blank or incomplete?

A thin or empty service section usually means the shops that maintained the car don't report, not that the car was neglected. Service records come mainly from franchise dealerships and some large chains, which participate voluntarily; most independent mechanics and body shops feed no history-report network, so a faithfully maintained car can show almost no service history. Read a blank section as missing data, not a red flag, and ask the seller for paper receipts and stamped maintenance books, which capture work no database ever saw.

What records are commonly missing from every report?

Every vehicle history report shares the same blind spots, regardless of provider, because they all depend on what gets reported. Commonly missing: cash or out-of-network accident repairs; work by non-reporting independent mechanics; unclaimed collisions and unreported minor damage; and private-party maintenance. Anything paid without an insurance claim, or handled by a shop outside the reporting networks, can be absent everywhere. This is why a "clean" report is a floor, not a guarantee — it confirms nothing adverse was reported, not that nothing happened. What to verify by VIN before you pay

  1. Open recalls — run the VIN free at nhtsa.gov/recalls; if any are open, ask the dealer for proof the repair was done (the report shows presence, not remedy).
  2. Legal title brand — confirm the current brand at NMVTIS (vehiclehistory.gov) and the issuing state's DMV; don't rely on a commercial report's salvage-auction line alone.
  3. Accident and airbag-deployment records — read location, type, severity and airbag status; treat a blank section as missing data, not a guarantee.
  4. Odometer timeline — compare the summary against the detailed readings and resolve any gap or "possible rollback" flag.
  5. Ownership and title events — check owner count, states and title-issued dates for a pattern that doesn't fit the story you were told.
  6. Theft — screen the VIN free at NICB VinCheck for a stolen-and-unrecovered flag.
