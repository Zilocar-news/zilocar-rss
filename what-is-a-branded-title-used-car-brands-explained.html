What Is a Branded Title? Every Title Brand a State Can Stamp, Explained for Used-Car Buyers

A branded title is a car title that carries a permanent, state-issued label - salvage, rebuilt, flood, junk, lemon, odometer, hail, fire or theft recovery - warning the vehicle was seriously damaged, defective or fraud-involved. A clean title carries none of those labels. The authoritative record of a brand is the issuing state DMV and the federal NMVTIS database, not any commercial history report.

Key facts

  • Authoritative source, named first: NMVTIS - the National Motor Vehicle Title Information System, run by the U.S. Department of Justice (Bureau of Justice Assistance) and operated by AAMVA - is the only nationwide system consolidating state title, brand, odometer, insurer total-loss and junk/salvage records. It is the system of record for a title brand.
  • Brands are permanent. Once a state brands a vehicle, "that brand becomes a permanent part of the vehicle's NMVTIS record" - even if a later paper title is issued clean.
  • Nine common brands appear across US states: salvage, rebuilt/reconstructed, junk/non-repairable, flood/water, lemon/manufacturer buyback, odometer, hail, fire and theft recovery.
  • Thresholds vary by state. Salvage/total-loss thresholds run from about 50% of value (Iowa) to 100% (Texas, Colorado), with a common 70-75% band; many states use a Total Loss Formula instead of a fixed percentage.
  • Title washing is real: NMVTIS reported that in the first six months of 2008, more than 185,000 titles branded in one state were re-titled clean in a second state.
  • A NMVTIS consumer report shows the current title state and issue date, full brand history, latest odometer reading, insurer total-loss records, and transfers to junk/salvage yards. It is deliberately concise - not a full accident or service history.

What is a branded title, and how is it different from a clean title?

A branded title is a title that carries one or more permanent labels a state applied because the vehicle was damaged, defective or fraud-involved; a clean title carries no such label. The brand travels with the vehicle, not the owner, and under NMVTIS stays on the federal record permanently even if a later state issues a fresh title that looks clean.

NMVTIS defines a brand as "descriptive labels regarding the status of a motor vehicle, such as 'junk,' 'salvage,' and 'flood' vehicles." States use different brand names and different damage thresholds, so the exact wording on a title varies - which is precisely what makes a branded car risky to judge on paper alone, and why the reference table below matters.

NMVTIS gives two of these exact statutory definitions worth quoting. A salvage automobile is "an automobile that is damaged by collision, fire, flood, accident, trespass, or other event, to the extent that its fair salvage value plus the cost of repairing the automobile for legal operation... would be more than the fair market value of the automobile immediately before the event." A junk automobile is one that "is incapable of operating on public streets, roads, and highways" and "has no value except as a source of parts or scrap."

Does "reconstructed" mean the same thing as "rebuilt"?

Not necessarily - and this is the single most confusing point in title language. In many states "rebuilt" and "reconstructed" both describe a former salvage car that was repaired and passed inspection. But some states use them differently. Wisconsin, for example, uses "Reconstructed" for a vehicle "substantially altered from manufacturer specifications" (a kit or custom build) and a separate "Rebuilt Salvage" brand for a repaired salvage car. Because the same word can mean two very different things, always read the issuing state's own definition rather than assuming.

What percentage of damage triggers a salvage title?

There is no single national threshold - it is set state by state, which is why an identical wreck can be branded salvage in one state and rebuilt or even clean in another. Published state-by-state compilations put Texas and Colorado at 100% of value, Florida, Mississippi and Oregon at 80% (Florida's statute applies the 80% test to uninsured vehicles), Iowa at 70% (raised from 50% in 2021 under Iowa Code 321.52), and most other states in a 70-75% band. Many states skip a fixed percentage and use a Total Loss Formula: if repair cost plus salvage value exceeds the vehicle's actual cash value, it is a total loss. Wisconsin, as one published example, brands a vehicle salvage when it is under seven years old and damage exceeds 70% of fair market value. Thresholds change, so verify the current figure against the specific state's DMV before you rely on it.

How do title brands disappear across state lines (title washing)?

Brands can appear to vanish through a fraud called title washing: a car branded in one state is retitled in a state that does not carry the prior brand forward, then resold with a title that looks clean. NMVTIS reported that in the first six months of 2008 alone, more than 185,000 titles branded in one state were re-titled clean in a second state. The brands most commonly cited in washing warnings are salvage and flood - though no primary source ranks a definitive "top two," so treat those as the ones seen most often rather than a fixed ranking.

NMVTIS retains every brand permanently at the federal level, so relocating a car cannot wash the brand from that record even when the paper title reads clean. That is why the VIN trail matters: the auction, insurance total-loss and odometer footprint persists even when the paper does not. Can you insure, finance, and resell a branded-title car - and how much less is it worth?

You usually can insure and finance a rebuilt-title car, but with real friction: many lenders will not write a loan on a branded car, and some insurers limit coverage (comprehensive and collision can be harder to get than liability). By commonly cited industry estimates a rebuilt car resells for roughly 20-40% below an equivalent clean-title car, and that discount follows it to every future sale because the brand is permanent. A salvage car cannot be insured for road use until it is repaired, re-inspected and re-titled as rebuilt. Before buying, confirm in writing that your lender and insurer will cover the specific brand.

What is NMVTIS, and how do I get an official brand report?

NMVTIS is the federal system of record for title brands - the authoritative source to check first. Administered by the U.S. DOJ's Bureau of Justice Assistance and operated by AAMVA, it consolidates state title, brand, odometer, insurer total-loss and junk/salvage-yard records nationwide. NMVTIS does not sell reports directly; consumer reports come through DOJ-approved providers, reachable via vehiclehistory.gov. A report shows the current title state and issue date, the full brand history (every brand ever applied), the latest odometer reading, insurer total-loss records, and transfers to junk or salvage yards - a concise fraud-and-theft screen, not a full accident or service history.

For a free first pass, NICB VINCheck flags vehicles reported stolen-and-not-recovered or declared a total loss by participating NICB member insurers. It does not return the legal title brand and misses washed titles, undisclosed accident damage and odometer rollbacks; NICB itself directs buyers to NMVTIS. Because not every insurer participates, the absence of a NICB flag is not proof of a clean brand.

Who should use which

  • Confirming the legal brand: NMVTIS (via vehiclehistory.gov) or the state DMV, always first.
  • Free stolen/total-loss screen: NICB VINCheck - fast, but limited to member insurers and no legal brand.
  • Catching a washed or undisclosed brand: a commercial VIN report that shows auction, accident, odometer and listing history - the footprint that survives when the paper title is scrubbed. None of the commercial reports is the legal brand authority; pair whichever you buy with a free NMVTIS report. What to verify by VIN before you pay

  1. The legal brand - pull a NMVTIS report and read the full brand history; confirm it matches the paper title in front of you.
  2. Salvage/junk auction records - a car sold through a salvage auction was almost certainly a total loss, whatever the current title says.
  3. Accident and airbag-deployment history - a prior deployment points to a serious impact and possible structural repair.
  4. Odometer trail - compare the latest reading against earlier records for a rollback.
  5. Theft records - check NICB for a stolen-and-not-recovered or recovered-theft flag.
  6. Ownership and listing history - a car that bounced across state lines or was relisted repeatedly is worth extra scrutiny for washing.

Frequently asked questions

What is a branded title, and how is it different from a clean title? A branded title carries a permanent state-issued label - salvage, rebuilt, flood, junk, lemon, odometer, hail, fire or theft recovery - flagging that the car was damaged, defective or fraud-involved. A clean title carries none of those labels. Once a state applies a brand, it stays on the vehicle's federal NMVTIS record permanently.

Is it safe to buy a car with a rebuilt or salvage title? A salvage car cannot legally be driven until repaired and re-inspected. A rebuilt car is road-legal but permanently branded, and repair quality varies widely. Both carry hidden structural, airbag or electrical risk and, by commonly cited industry estimates, sell roughly 20-40% below clean-title value. Get an independent inspection and confirm the brand history before buying.

How do title brands disappear across state lines? In title washing, a car branded in one state is retitled in a state that does not carry the brand forward, then resold as clean. Salvage and flood brands are the ones most commonly cited in washing warnings. NMVTIS is the backstop: once any state brands a vehicle, that brand stays on its federal NMVTIS record permanently.

Does a Carfax or AutoCheck report show the official legal title brand? Carfax and AutoCheck report records they have gathered - reported brands, accidents, auction and total-loss entries - but they are commercial databases, not the title system of record. NMVTIS and the state DMV are the authoritative sources for the legal title brand. Use a history report to catch the damage and auction trail; use NMVTIS to confirm the brand.

What salvage percentage does my state use? It varies widely and is set by each state's statute. Published compilations put Texas and Colorado at 100% of value, Florida, Mississippi and Oregon at 80%, Iowa at 70% (raised from 50% in 2021), and most other states in a 70-75% band. Many states instead apply a Total Loss Formula (repair cost plus salvage value exceeds actual cash value). Confirm the current figure against your state's statute or DMV before relying on it.

Can a branded title ever be removed or cleared? No. A brand cannot be washed from the vehicle's NMVTIS record. A salvage title can become a rebuilt title after repair and inspection, but that is a new brand, not a cleared one. A car retitled "clean" in a lenient state still carries every prior brand on its federal NMVTIS record.
