Does New York have a free online title and lien check by VIN?

Yes. Unlike states that only answer records requests by mail, New York publishes a free online NYS title check. The NY DMV Title/Lien Status Check at dmv.ny.gov/how-check-title-or-lien-status lets anyone look up vehicle, boat and manufactured-home title and lien status at no charge. You enter three things — the VIN (or a boat's HIN, or a manufactured home's serial number), the model year, and the make — and the tool returns the record on screen.

What it displays is narrow but authoritative for New York: the date the DMV issued the title, the number of liens on the record if any, and the name and address of each lienholder. It deliberately shows no personal information about the owner or registrant. That makes it the single best free tool for one specific question a used-car buyer needs answered before money changes hands: is there still a lender or lienholder with a claim on this car?

What does the NY DMV title/lien status check show — and what does it leave out?

The NY DMV check is authoritative for the live New York lien and the title-issue record, and blind to almost everything else. It is the live lien authority for New York, but it is not a vehicle-history report: it holds no odometer trail, no accident data, no auction records, and no out-of-state history. How do I run the NYS title/lien check step by step?

Running the check takes under a minute and needs no account. Open dmv.ny.gov/how-check-title-or-lien-status, follow through to the Title/Lien Status Check tool, and enter the vehicle's 17-character VIN, its model year, and its make. Submit, and the New York title record appears: the title issue date, the number of liens, and any lienholder's name and address.

Read the result for two things. First, the lien count. Zero liens means no lender or lienholder is recorded against the car in New York — the clean answer you want. One or more liens means someone besides the seller has a claim, and you should not pay until it is resolved or you understand exactly how it will be. Second, the lienholder name and address, which tells you who to contact to confirm a payoff. Because the tool is the live New York authority, a lien that still shows here has not been released in the DMV's records, whatever a seller tells you verbally. What the check will not tell you is anything about the car's condition or past — for accidents, mileage and auction history you still need a paid VIN report, and this same split shows up in every state, including our companion florida title check guide.

How do I know if a lien has been paid off or released on a NY title?

A New York lien is printed on the paper certificate of title, and it stays there until the DMV removes it. To clear a satisfied lien, the owner mails three things to NYS DMV Title Services, 6 Empire State Plaza, Albany, NY 12228-0331: (1) original proof the lien was satisfied — a signed Notice of Recorded Lien (MV-901) or a lienholder letter signed by an authorized officer (photocopies are not accepted); (2) the original current title certificate; and (3) a $20.00 check or money order payable to "Commissioner of Motor Vehicles." The DMV then mails a clean, lien-free title in roughly 60–90 days as of 2026-09-30.

Here is the part that matters most to a buyer: when selling, the owner does not have to remove the lien first. New York lets the seller hand the buyer the original title plus the original lien release. So a car can legally change hands with a satisfied-but-not-yet-removed lien, as long as you get that original release document. The safe move is to run the free NY DMV Title/Lien Status Check yourself and, if a lien still shows, insist on the original lien-release paperwork before you pay — do not accept a photocopy or a verbal assurance. Confirming the paper trail is part of the same discipline as how to verify a used car title is clean before buying.

What is a salvage or rebuilt title in New York, and what is the 75% threshold?

New York brands a car's title by condition, and the trigger is a percentage. Under 15 NYCRR 20.20, a vehicle that is 8 model years old or newer and whose estimated or actual rebuild cost exceeds 75% of its undamaged value must be branded Rebuilt Salvage. That 75% figure is New York's total-loss/salvage threshold. A rebuilt-salvage vehicle returning to the road must first pass a DMV anti-theft/salvage examination under the NY Auto Theft Prevention Program before a new title and registration issue. For how that brand follows a car through resale and insurance, see what is a salvage title.

Out-of-state brands carry over onto the New York title: labels such as RECONSTRUCTED or NON-USA-STD stay on the record, and equivalents like "salvage," "rebuilt," "junk," "parts only" and "water damage" are treated as salvage. Vehicles marked "parts only," "non-rebuildable," "non-repairable," "destroyed" or "scrapped" cannot return to the road or get a new NY title at all. The DMV's free title/lien check confirms the title record but not this brand detail — for the official brand you need an NMVTIS report or an MV-15 certified title record, and what is a branded title lists every brand a state can stamp onto a title.

Does NMVTIS show liens, and is it the same as a NY DMV lien check?

No — these are two different authorities, and confusing them is a common mistake. NMVTIS, the National Motor Vehicle Title Information System run under the U.S. DOJ Bureau of Justice Assistance, is the federal title-history system. It is the authority for title brands, brand history across states, odometer readings, and junk/salvage/insurance total-loss records, plus the current state of title. That is what you use to catch a brand New York inherited from another state.

NMVTIS is not a reliable live lien source. Its lien coverage is inconsistent across providers — standard NMVTIS is a title-brand system and does not dependably carry lienholder contact information; where a provider shows active-lien data it often comes from separate national sources, not core NMVTIS. So treat the two tools by their strengths: use the free NY DMV Title/Lien Status Check for the live New York lien and lienholder, and use NMVTIS (through an approved consumer provider) or an MV-15 certified title record for the official title brand. Consumers cannot buy NMVTIS reports directly from dealer-only channels; several approved consumer providers sell them.

Who should use which

Buying a car titled in New York and you mainly need the lien answer: run the free NY DMV Title/Lien Status Check first. It is the live New York authority for liens and lienholders and costs nothing.

You want the official title record or the legal brand on paper: order an MV-15 certified title record ($7 electronic / $10 mailed, as of 2026-09-30), or pull an NMVTIS report through an approved provider for the brand and brand history across states.

You want the car's damage, odometer and ownership history — not just title status: you need a paid VIN history report. The free NY check shows no accidents, no mileage trail and no auction records; a report is where that evidence lives, and it is the layer that catches a title-washed brand New York never recorded.

What to verify by VIN before you pay in New York

  1. Lien status — run the free NY DMV Title/Lien Status Check; confirm zero liens, or get the original lien-release document for any lien that still shows.
  2. Lienholder identity — note the lienholder name and address the tool returns, and contact them to confirm a payoff if a lien appears.
  3. Official title brand — pull NMVTIS or an MV-15 certified title record for the legal brand and any out-of-state brand that carried over.
  4. Salvage / rebuilt history — check for junk/salvage auction records and the Rebuilt Salvage brand; New York's threshold is rebuild cost over 75% of undamaged value.
  5. Odometer and accidents — pull a paid VIN report for the mileage history, rollback screening and accident/airbag-deployment records the free check never shows.
  6. Ownership and listing trail — review prior owners and past sales listings for a story that doesn't add up before you commit.
