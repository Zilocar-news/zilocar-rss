In 2026, 44 US car and light-truck recalls told owners their vehicle was defective before the repair existed — 12.9% of the 341 campaigns filed through 19 November, covering 5,254,959 vehicles. The largest landed two days ago: Honda's 810,592-vehicle Takata inflator recall, whose interim letters go out on 18 December and whose real repair letters follow "once the final remedy is available."

Key facts

  • 44 of 341 2026 car and light-truck recall campaigns (12.9%) sent an interim letter before the remedy was ready, covering 5,254,959 vehicles of 36,153,105 recalled.
  • A second, independent wording test returns 50 campaigns and 6,141,841 vehicles; 43 campaigns satisfy both, so the two definitions largely confirm each other.
  • Ford: 19 of 85 campaigns (22.4%), against 3.7% at General Motors and 0 of 16 at BMW. The gap survives controlling for recall size and for filing date.
  • The size gradient is real and large: 2.9% in the smallest fifth of recalls, 20.3% in the largest. Median interim campaign covers 43,175 vehicles against 8,230 for the rest.
  • The year-over-year story does not hold up. Whole-year rates suggest 2026 is four times any year since 2015. On a matched January–June window it is the highest of eleven years but only 1.27x the 2016 high. We report the failed check below.
  • A VIN check shows that an open recall exists; it cannot show whether a remedy has been released. These 44 campaigns are exactly where that distinction bites.

What does it mean when a car is recalled before the fix exists?

It means the manufacturer has accepted the car is defective and is legally required to tell owners now, even though it cannot yet tell them what to do about it. Under 49 CFR 577.7, when a remedy is not available at the time notification is due, the manufacturer must send an interim letter describing the defect and the risk, and then a second letter once the repair is ready.

For an owner, the interim letter is the awkward one. It carries safety instructions rather than a repair appointment: park away from structures, limit the charge, stop using a seat or a feature. There is no legal deadline by which the second letter must arrive.

Honda's 26V765000, filed on 19 November 2026 and the largest such campaign of the year, reads exactly this way: dealers will replace the front driver or passenger airbag inflator, "interim letters notifying owners of the safety risk are expected to be mailed December 18, 2026," and "additional letters will be sent once the final remedy is available."

The contrast is instructive. Toyota and Lexus's 26V748000, 314,029 vehicles filed a week earlier over the same class of Takata inflator defect, carries no interim letter — its owner letters are expected from 28 December with the repair already specified. Two neighbouring airbag recalls, one with a fix and one without.

Are recalls without a ready fix becoming more common? The check that failed

This is where our own first answer was wrong, and the correction matters more than the original claim.

Counted by whole year, 2026's 12.9% towers over every year back to 2015, where the rate ran between 0.3% and 4.1%. That reads as a four-fold deterioration and it is the number we would have published.

It is an artifact of when a campaign was filed. The corrective-action text is written at filing and revised when the remedy ships, so a recall filed last month is far likelier to still say "once the final remedy is available" than one filed three years ago. The gradient is visible inside every year:

How old are the cars in these recalls?

Mostly late-model, so this is a problem for first and second owners more than for cheap used stock. Across the 44 campaigns, the oldest model year covered sits a median of four years before the filing date. But eight of the 44 reach back a decade or more and the furthest reaches 15 model years — Honda's November inflator campaign among them, which is why a Takata recall can still land on a car old enough to be on its fourth owner.

By component family, the 44 cluster in structure and electrical faults rather than in any single system: structure 8, electrical system 8, airbags 4, back-over prevention 4, power train 4, seat belts 3, suspension 3.

What a VIN check can and cannot tell you here

This is the honest boundary, and this study sits right on it.

A Zilocar VIN check can tell you:

  • That an open recall exists on the car, and how many.
  • The car's ownership and sales-listing history, so you can see how long the current owner has had it.
  • Accident and damage records, including airbag-deployment records — relevant if a previous deployment involved a now-recalled inflator.
  • Salvage and junk auction records, odometer readings and rollback flags, theft records (NICB), specs, NHTSA and IIHS ratings and a valuation.

It cannot tell you:

  • Whether a remedy has been released for that campaign. No vehicle history report carries this.
  • Whether this particular car has had the repair done, or a software recall flashed.
  • Whether the owner received an interim letter rather than a repair letter.
  • The status of an NHTSA investigation (a PE or EA number) that has not yet become a recall.

For all four, the free sources are authoritative and we cede them: NHTSA's VIN lookup for open recalls and remedy status on that specific VIN, and a franchised dealer for that brand for whether the work was carried out. Use the VIN check for history and screening, use NHTSA and the dealer for remedy state.

Method

Source file. NHTSA's Part 573 recall flat file, https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/ffdd/rcl/FLAT_RCL_POST_2010.zip, retrieved 2026-11-21 at 08:03 UTC: HTTP 200, 14,907,455 bytes, Last-Modified: Sat, 21 Nov 2026 08:03:58 GMT, ZipFile.testzip() returned None so the archive is intact. At retrieval the file held 248,584 rows and 15,424 distinct campaign numbers. Freshness assertions: max(RCDATE) across all record types = 20261119; max(RCDATE) restricted to RCLTYPECD='V' = 20261119; and both campaigns at that frontier (26V765000 Honda/Acura, 26V756000 Ram) are passenger vehicles, not equipment or utility trucks. We check the last point explicitly because a V record type means "vehicle", not "car" — on two earlier runs the frontier was set by aerial-lift utility trucks and by an equipment campaign, overstating freshness by one and four days respectively.

Universe. The file carries one row per campaign-component-model combination, so it was collapsed to one record per campaign number, keeping the campaign-level fields (POTAFF, MFGTXT, RCDATE, CORRECTIVE_ACTION, NOTES) and unioning makes, models, model years and components. We then kept campaigns with RCLTYPECD='V' and RCDATE in calendar 2026, filed by a car or light-truck manufacturer. Manufacturer scope uses a normalised-substring allow-list rather than exact MFGTXT matching, because MFGTXT is not canonical — the same filer appears under several strings, and an exact-match list silently dropped the largest campaign of an earlier study. The resulting universe is 341 campaigns and 36,153,105 potentially affected units.

Classification. A campaign is counted as notified before remedy under either of two independent text tests applied to CORRECTIVE_ACTION concatenated with NOTES:

  1. Interim-letter test — the text contains an interim-notification phrase. This is the regulatory marker: 49 CFR 577.7 requires an interim notification precisely when the remedy is not available when notification falls due. 44 campaigns, 5,254,959 units.
  2. Explicit-wording test — the text states that the remedy or parts are not yet available, are being developed, or that further letters will follow once the remedy is available. 50 campaigns, 6,141,841 units.

43 campaigns satisfy both; 1 is interim-only and 7 explicit-only. All headline figures use test 1, the narrower and regulatorily-grounded one.

Reversal checks. Four were run; three were survived and one failed.

  1. Recall size. Rate by size quintile rises monotonically, 2.9% → 8.8% → 14.7% → 17.6% → 20.3%, and the median interim campaign is 5.2x the size of the median non-interim campaign. Size is a strong confounder and is reported as a finding in its own right.
  2. Manufacturer within size. Ford vs all other filers, computed separately inside the largest quintile (35.0% vs 14.3%) and across the smallest four (18.5% vs 8.7%). Ford is ~2x in both strata, so the Ford result is not a size artifact.
  3. Manufacturer within filing half. Ford vs others in January–June (12.7% vs 6.5%) and July–November (40.0% vs 14.9%) separately. Ford's share of its own filings falling in H1 is 64.7% against 61.6% for the universe, so Ford is not date-skewed either.
  4. Year-over-year — FAILED, and the headline was rewritten because of it. Whole-year rates make 2026 (12.9%) roughly 4x the 2015–2025 range (0.3%–4.1%). That is an artifact of text staleness: the rate climbs steeply with filing date inside every year (2026 H1 8.1% vs H2 20.6%; 2025 H1 1.3% vs H2 5.3%), because the corrective-action text is written at filing and revised when the remedy ships. On a matched January–June window across eleven years, 2026's 8.1% is the highest on record but only 1.27x the previous high (2016, 6.4%) and about 3.4x the eleven-year H1 mean of 2.3%. No four-fold claim is made anywhere on this page, and the matched figure is the one to cite.

Known limits.

  • Both tests are free-text screens, not a structured NHTSA field. There is no remedyAvailable flag in the file; if there were, this analysis would be unnecessary. Wording varies between filers, and a manufacturer with a house style that omits the phrase would be undercounted. This is the single largest source of error and it is not quantifiable from the file alone.
  • Text is revised over time. A campaign whose remedy later shipped may have had its interim language removed, which is the mechanism behind the failed check above. The file therefore measures current text, not the state at filing, and the two diverge more the older the campaign.
  • 2026 is incomplete (through 19 November). This does not bias a rate, but it does make the whole-2026 figure non-comparable with settled years, as above.
  • POTAFF is a manufacturer estimate at filing and is not revised downward as vehicles are scrapped, so unit totals overstate the cars actually on the road today.
  • Per-filer rates for everyone except Ford, FCA, Toyota, General Motors and Hyundai rest on fewer than 25 campaigns, and Jaguar Land Rover's leading rate rests on 15. Treat the small-N rows as a spread, not a league table.
  • Campaigns covering several makes are counted once, under their filer, so Ford/Lincoln campaigns sit in the Ford row.
