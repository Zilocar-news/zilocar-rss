Priced Out at $30K, Used-Car Buyers Are Fleeing to 7-to-10-Year-Old Cars: What Those Older Cars Hide

TL;DR: The average U.S. used-car price hit $30,200 in May 2026, up 5.1% year-over-year and its first crossing above $30,000 since August 2023 (not an all-time high). Priced-out buyers are shifting to older cars: 7-to-10-year-old vehicles grew from 17% of used sales in 2020 to 23% in mid-2026. A VIN check can surface an older car's accident, salvage-auction, odometer and ownership history and screen recall presence, but it cannot confirm a recall was remedied.

Key facts

  • Average used-car price: $30,200 in May 2026, up 5.1% YoY, the first crossing above $30,000 since August 2023 (CarGurus monthly index, via CBT News).
  • Average new-car price: $50,700 in May 2026, up 1.7% YoY, its highest since September 2023.
  • The migration: 7-to-10-year-old vehicles rose from 17% of used sales in 2020 to 23% in mid-2026 (CarGurus Intelligence Report, Mid-Year 2026).
  • Broader cut: vehicles 7 years old and older grew from 32% to 40% of used sales.
  • Higher mileage: sales of vehicles with 60,000-150,000 miles are up 16% versus 2020.
  • Trucks illustrate it: used Ford F-150, Chevrolet Silverado 1500 and RAM 1500 average roughly $20,000 despite topping 120,000 miles.
  • Depreciation eased: iSeeCars puts average five-year depreciation at 41.8% in 2026, improved from 45.6% in 2025; EVs depreciate most (57.2%), trucks least (34.2%).
  • The catch: older, higher-mileage cars have had more years to accumulate accident, salvage, odometer and multi-owner history than the near-new cars buyers left behind.

Why are used-car prices over $30,000 in 2026, and where are priced-out buyers going?

Affordability pressure is pushing buyers down the age curve. The average used car hit $30,200 in May 2026, up 5.1% year-over-year, while the average new car reached $50,700. With new inventory increasingly stacked above $50,000, buyers are chasing lower prices in older stock: 7-to-10-year-old vehicles grew from 17% of used sales in 2020 to 23% in mid-2026, and sales of 60,000-150,000-mile cars are up 16% since 2020.

The shift is structural, not seasonal. In 2020, over half of new inventory was priced below $35,000; by 2026 there is more new inventory above $50,000 than below $35,000. CarGurus' Used Vehicle Demand Index reached 117.8 (up 4.1% month-over-month, up 3.2% year-over-year), confirming that demand is holding even as prices climb. Trucks are the clearest example of the trade-off: used F-150, Silverado 1500 and RAM 1500 models average roughly $20,000 while exceeding 120,000 miles on the odometer.

Is a 7-to-10-year-old car with over 100,000 miles a smart buy, or a trap?

It can be a smart buy, but only after you verify the car's history. Older trucks and hybrids retain value well and can deliver real savings, which is exactly why buyers are moving into them. The trap is that an affordability win can hide a history cost: a car built 7 to 10 years ago has had years more time to accumulate reported accidents, salvage-auction passes, odometer discrepancies and multiple owners than the near-new car a buyer was originally shopping.

That is the migration's blind spot. The same features that make an older car cheap, more time on the road and more miles, also make it more likely to carry events that a listing photo will not show. The fix is not to avoid older cars; it is to run the VIN and read the record before you commit.

Do older cars depreciate less, and which hold value best?

Depreciation actually eased in 2026, which helps explain why older cars are attractive. According to iSeeCars' "Cars That Hold Their Value" study (published March 24, 2026; more than 950,000 five-year-old used cars analyzed), average five-year depreciation was 41.8%, an improvement of 3.8 points from 45.6% in 2025. That means recent model years are retaining value better than near-new cars did a year earlier, sharpening the case for buying used.

Retention varies sharply by powertrain. Trucks depreciate least at 34.2% and hybrids at 35.4%, while electric vehicles lose the most at 57.2%. The best-retention models are performance and Japanese nameplates: the Porsche 718 Cayman (9.6% five-year loss), Porsche 911 (11.1%) and Chevrolet Corvette (18.7%), with Toyota holding 10 of the top 25 spots. At the other extreme, the Nissan LEAF depreciates 63.1% and the Land Rover Range Rover 61.7%. (iSeeCars data, labeled separately from the CarGurus market figures; the 57.2% EV figure is a segment average and should not be conflated with the LEAF's 63.1% worst-model result.)

What should I check before buying an older, high-mileage used car?

Before buying an older, high-mileage car, verify five categories of history by VIN: accident and damage records (including airbag-deployment status), junk and salvage auction records, odometer readings for rollback, the number of previous owners, and past sales-listing history. These are the events most likely to have accumulated over a 7-to-10-year life, and none of them are visible in a listing photo or a quick test drive.

Run the checks in this order. First, use NHTSA's free VIN recall lookup at NHTSA.gov, the authoritative source that shows whether a recall has been remedied. Second, pull a VIN-based vehicle history report, such as a Zilocar VIN check, to surface accident and airbag-deployment records, salvage and junk auction records, odometer/rollback flags, theft records, ownership count, and sales-listing history (prices, mileage and days-on-market, the strongest signal for spotting a flipped or long-sitting car). Third, sanity-check the asking price against a market valuation so you are not overpaying the older-car premium. Finally, get an independent pre-purchase mechanical inspection, which is the only way to judge current mechanical condition.

What a VIN check can and can't tell you here

A full VIN-based vehicle history report is directly on-point for this story because a 7-to-10-year-old car has had years more time to accumulate history. It surfaces reported history and screens recall presence, but it is not a substitute for NHTSA on remedy status or the state DMV on title brand. Frequently asked questions

What should I check before buying an older, high-mileage used car? Check accident and damage records (including airbag deployment), odometer readings for rollback, junk and salvage auction records, theft records, number of previous owners, and past sales-listing history. Also run the free NHTSA VIN recall lookup and get a pre-purchase mechanical inspection.

Is a 7-to-10-year-old car with over 100,000 miles a smart buy in 2026? It can be. Trucks and hybrids retain value well, and 60,000-150,000-mile sales are up 16% versus 2020. The risk is hidden history: older cars have had more years to accumulate accident, salvage, odometer and multi-owner records, so verify by VIN before buying.

How do I find out if a used car has been in an accident or had airbag deployment? A VIN-based vehicle history report can surface reported accident and damage records, including location, type, severity, and airbag-deployment status. A history report reflects only reported events, so pair it with a pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic.

How can I tell if a car's odometer has been rolled back? A VIN check compares odometer readings recorded over the car's life; a later reading lower than an earlier one signals a likely rollback. It surfaces these odometer records and rollback flags, but confirm the mileage against service and inspection records.

What is a salvage or junk auction record and how do I check for one by VIN? A junk or salvage auction record shows a vehicle passed through a salvage or junk auction, often after major damage. A VIN check surfaces these auction records. Note it shows the auction record, not the legal title-brand classification, which you confirm with the state DMV or title.

Does the car have any open recalls, and how do I check by VIN for free? Check recalls free at NHTSA.gov using the VIN; it is the authoritative source and shows remedy status. A VIN check screens recall presence and count like NHTSA's tool but does not confirm whether a recall was remedied, so verify remedy status with NHTSA and the dealer.

Do older cars depreciate less, and which hold value best? Per iSeeCars, average five-year depreciation eased to 41.8% in 2026 from 45.6% in 2025. Trucks (34.2%) and hybrids (35.4%) retain value best, while EVs depreciate most at 57.2%. Porsche and Toyota models dominate the strongest-retention rankings.
