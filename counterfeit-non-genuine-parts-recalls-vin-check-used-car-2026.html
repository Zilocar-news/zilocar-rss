Counterfeit and Non-Genuine Parts in US Recalls: Why a VIN Recall Check Can Miss the Part Actually on Your Car

**Across 13,642 US vehicle-recall campaigns filed from 2010 to 2026-09-24, the words counterfeit, non-genuine, uncertified and fraudulent appear zero times in the official defect and corrective-action text — a census we computed over NHTSA's recalls flat file (218,820 vehicle rows). Even the 2026 Ford Ranger rearview-camera recall behind this week's "counterfeit" headlines never uses the word. A VIN recall check proves a recall exists, not what part is physically on the car.**

Do NHTSA recalls actually use the word "counterfeit"?

No. We searched the defect, consequence and corrective-action text of every US vehicle recall from 2010 through 2026-09-24 — 13,642 campaigns, 218,820 rows — for counterfeit, non-genuine (and nongenuine / "not genuine"), uncertified, and fraudulent. Each of those four terms returns exactly zero campaigns. Extending the search to all recall types (equipment, tire, child restraint) leaves the count at zero. The vocabulary buyers now hear in the news is effectively absent from 16 years of official Part 573 recall filings.

That silence is not evidence the problem doesn't exist. Recalls are what a manufacturer files when it identifies a safety defect in its own supply chain. A fake capacitor sold on a marketplace, or a salvaged airbag installed by a body shop after a crash, never enters that filing system — which is exactly why the recall record is quiet on counterfeit parts. This is the same blind spot behind how to check if a used car has a fake or aftermarket airbag: the danger lives in the aftermarket, not the recall bulletin.

Is the count of "counterfeit" recalls rising?

No — it is flat at zero and, for the closest adjacent term, falling. The four hard terms register zero every single year from 2010 to 2026. The only word that produces any matches is suspect, and it appears in 41 campaigns whose language is routine quality control ("dealers will replace the suspect fuel pump," "any switch from the suspect lot"), not counterfeiting. Even taken at face value, those 41 campaigns decline as a share of all vehicle recalls: 1.24% in 2010, 1.17% in 2011, 0.86% in 2012, then 0.00% in 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026-to-date. The trend is robust to the keyword set — zero for counterfeit-only, and falling for the broader counterfeit-plus-suspect set.

Why did Ford recall the 2026 Ranger — and was it really counterfeit parts?

NHTSA campaign 26V605000 (Ford recall 26C42), filed 2026-09-22, covers 11,405 model-year 2026 Ford Rangers equipped with the 10.1-inch center-stack screen. Per the official filing, "the screen may lose power and fail to display the rearview camera image," which makes the vehicles non-compliant with Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 111, "Rear Visibility." A related equipment recall, 26E065000, covers 46 service-replacement display units for 2024-2026 Rangers. As of 2026-10-09, Ford's remedy remains under development; the interim owner letters were scheduled to mail 2026-10-05 — a date that has now passed, with a follow-up letter to come once a remedy is available — and affected VINs became searchable on NHTSA.gov from 2026-09-25.

The word "counterfeit" appears nowhere in NHTSA's filing. Trade coverage (autoevolution, and Yahoo's headline, which uses "suspect parts") attributes the black screen to a cracked multi-layer ceramic capacitor on the display board — possibly counterfeit and sourced from overseas — that blows the power fuse. That supplier-quality detail is media reporting, not part of the Part 573 text we can verify. Fox Business and the Detroit Free Press describe this as Ford's "77th recall of 2026"; our file, which cuts off at 2026-09-24, shows 76 Ford campaigns of all types this year, consistent with a 77th filed just after the cutoff. Ford's recall volume is a recurring theme — see used ford recalls to check before buying for how to screen any used Ford by VIN.

What a VIN check can and can't tell you here

A VIN recall lookup — whether NHTSA's free tool or a paid history report — answers one question: does a recall campaign exist for this VIN? It cannot inspect the car or authenticate a part. That is the precise limit this data quantifies. Because zero recall campaigns describe counterfeit parts, and because the specific fake capacitor or salvaged airbag on any one car was fitted outside the recall system, no VIN lookup can surface it. What a history report can add is the pattern of events that tends to precede a fake or swapped part: a prior crash, an airbag deployment, or a trip through a salvage auction.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if a used car has counterfeit or non-genuine parts before I buy it? No VIN report can confirm it. A VIN check shows whether a recall exists and surfaces accident, airbag-deployment and salvage history — the red flags that precede fake parts. Confirm the part itself with a physical inspection and OEM-part documentation from a dealer or mechanic.

Does a VIN recall check show whether a fake or aftermarket part is physically on the car? No. A VIN recall lookup answers only whether a recall campaign exists for that VIN. It cannot see what part is bolted to the car, so a counterfeit capacitor, non-OEM screen or salvaged airbag installed outside any recall will not appear.

Can a VIN history report detect a counterfeit airbag or a non-OEM screen? No history report authenticates a specific part. What it can do is flag prior airbag deployment, salvage-auction records and accident history — the events after which counterfeit or salvaged parts are most often fitted. Treat those as prompts to inspect, not proof of a fake.

If a part was replaced with a cheap aftermarket copy after a crash, will any history report catch it? The replacement itself will not appear. The crash and any airbag deployment behind it often will. Use those records to demand proof that any replaced part is OEM and correctly installed, then verify with a mechanic.

Is a car with an open recall still safe to buy, and how do I confirm the recall was fixed? An open recall is common and usually fixable free at a franchised dealer. A VIN check shows a recall exists but not whether it was remedied. Confirm remedy status only through NHTSA's free VIN lookup or the manufacturer's dealer.

Where do counterfeit auto parts show up if not in the recall record? In the aftermarket supply chain — online marketplaces, salvage parts, and non-OEM replacements after collisions. Consumer-safety context (NPR, September 2026) describes marketplace bans and an NHTSA airbag-inflator investigation; those concerns rarely become recalls, so the recall file's silence means the paperwork is quiet, not that the problem is absent.

Method

Source file. NHTSA's Part 573 safety-recall flat file, https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/ffdd/rcl/FLAT_RCL_POST_2010.zip, retrieved 2026-09-29 07:24 UTC (archive member Last-Modified Tue, 29 Sep 2026 07:04:54 GMT). Tab-delimited, no header, 245,556 rows, every row exactly 29 fields. Freshness assert passed: the newest filing date in the file, max(RCDATE), is 2026-09-24, five days before retrieval — the only check that distinguishes a good NHTSA download from a silently truncated one. Cross-validated against the live recalls API: campaign 26V605000 returns 11,405 units and RCDATE 22/09/2026, matching the file exactly.

Universe. Campaigns with RCLTYPECD = 'V' (vehicle): 218,820 rows across 13,642 distinct campaigns, spanning 2010-01-01 to 2026-09-24. Rows are one per campaign x make x model x model-year x component label, so every figure here is deduplicated to the distinct campaign number and POTAFF is counted once per campaign.

Filter. Fields 20, 21 and 22 (DESC_DEFECT, CONEQUENCE_DEFECT, CORRECTIVE_ACTION) were concatenated and lowercased, then matched against the case-insensitive regex counterfeit|non-?genuine|not genuine|uncertified|fraudulent|suspect. Field numbers are 1-indexed as the file ships: 2=CAMPNO, 3=MAKETXT, 7=COMPNAME, 11=RCLTYPECD, 12=POTAFF, 16=RCDATE.

Result. counterfeit 0, non-genuine 0, uncertified 0, fraudulent 0. The only term that matches anything is suspect, at 41 campaigns covering 1,034,517 units. Across all recall types (V, E, T, C) the four hard terms are also literally zero.

The classification judgement, stated out loud. All 41 suspect matches are quality-control language for a suspected defective part or lot — "dealers will replace the suspect RRAB modules", "any switch found from the suspect lot" — and none reads as "suspected counterfeit". Counting them as counterfeit recalls would inflate the figure with routine defect recalls; excluding them returns the count to zero. Both readings are published, and every matched campaign is in the frozen CSV with the surrounding text so a reader can judge the call rather than take ours.

The reversal check. The claim most likely to break this story is "counterfeit-part recalls are rising". It does not survive either way it can be measured. The four hard terms are flat at zero in every year from 2010 to 2026. The closest adjacent term, suspect, falls as a share of all vehicle campaigns — 1.24% in 2010 (8 of 647), 1.17% in 2011, 0.86% in 2012, then 0.00% in 2016, 2019 and every year from 2022 to 2026. A second adjacent theme, aftermarket, appears in 31 campaigns (1,652,004 units) and is also flat, and on inspection describes aftermarket accessories and kits, not counterfeit parts.

Known limits.

  • A zero in the recall record is not a zero in the world. This census covers Part 573 recall

filings only, not NHTSA complaints or PE/EA investigations. Counterfeit-part activity that never becomes a recall is invisible here, and on the airbag evidence that is where most of it lives. The finding is that the recall system rarely names these parts, not that the parts are rare.

  • POTAFF is the manufacturer's launch-time estimate of units potentially affected. It is not a

count of cars still on the road, still unrepaired, or still in the US.

  • Absence of a word is not absence of a cause. A recall can be caused by a non-genuine component

and describe only the failure, which is exactly what campaign 26V605000 does.

  • "Counterfeit" in this week's coverage is the press's word, not NHTSA's. The Part 573 filing for

26V605000 cites only a display power loss and FMVSS 111.

  • One term we tested and discarded: imitation appears to match 44 lines, but every one is the

substring inside "limitation" ("weight limitation", "speed limitation"). The true count is zero.
