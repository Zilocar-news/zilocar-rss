The Airbag Blind Spot in Your VIN Recall Check: What a Counterfeit-Airbag Warning Means for Used-Car Buyers

Running a VIN through any recall search will not reveal a counterfeit or aftermarket replacement airbag, NHTSA warned on September 6, 2026. The dangerous DTN60DB inflators at the center of the warning were installed by third parties after a prior crash and are tied to no VIN or manufacturer campaign. A clean recall result does not mean the airbag is genuine or that it will deploy — only a physical inspection can confirm that.

Key facts

  • What it is: counterfeit and non-compliant frontal driver airbag inflators marked DTN60DB, made by Jilin Province Detiannuo Safety Technology Co. (DTN), China, in 2021-2022. Barcode labels read "144415654 666631" or "144415654 666633."
  • The danger: on deployment the inflator's metal housing can rupture, expelling shrapnel into the driver's chest, neck, eyes, and face in otherwise-survivable crashes. Counterfeits may also fail to deploy or inflate too slowly.
  • Casualties: 11 deaths and 3 severe injuries as of Sep 6, 2026 (up from 10 deaths and 2 severe injuries — 12 documented ruptures — at the April 2026 decision).
  • Models named: Chevrolet Malibu (2018-2022) and Hyundai Sonata (2017-2019) — but NHTSA warns the problem is not limited to these. An Aug 27, 2026 death in a 2018 Chevrolet Equinox (Dallas, TX) was the first outside that pattern.
  • How they got in: in at least 10 of 12 documented cases the DTN inflator was installed as an aftermarket replacement after a prior crash in which the original airbag had deployed.
  • No VIN list: this is an equipment defect order under EA25005 (49 U.S.C. 30118 / 49 CFR 554.10), not a Part 573 recall campaign. There is no campaign number and no list of affected vehicles.
  • Scale: roughly 10,000 DTN inflators are estimated to remain in U.S. vehicles; NHTSA stresses the true number is unknowable.
  • The remedy: a physical inspection of the airbag module by a certified technician. No VIN lookup can identify an affected vehicle.

How do I check if a used car has a fake or aftermarket airbag?

You cannot confirm a counterfeit airbag from a VIN alone — no tool can. NHTSA states plainly that these inflators "are not tied to individual VINs, and only an inspection can determine whether a vehicle has one." The honest buyer move is indirect: cross-reference the recall screen against the car's accident, airbag-deployment, and salvage-auction history, and let a mismatch drive you to a physical inspection.

The logic is simple. A counterfeit inflator only ends up in a car because someone replaced a deployed airbag. So the corroborating flag you are hunting for is evidence that the airbag was replaced at all: a prior airbag deployment, collision damage, or a salvage/junk-auction record. If those exist — especially if the seller says "no accidents" — the airbag module is exactly the part a certified technician should open and inspect before you hand over money. The same reasoning applies whether you are checking how to check if a used car had an airbag deployed after a genuine campaign or hunting for a fraudulent replacement part.

Why doesn't a VIN recall check show a counterfeit or aftermarket airbag?

A VIN recall search — NHTSA's free tool or any VIN report — screens for manufacturer recall campaigns tied to parts installed at the factory. Counterfeit DTN60DB inflators were installed by third parties after a wreck and are not tied to any VIN or manufacturer campaign, so the affected count NHTSA can identify by VIN is zero.

That is why the DTN case is not a normal recall. NHTSA opened Engineering Analysis EA25005 — its highest investigation tier — on Oct 21, 2025, published an Initial Decision in the Federal Register on Apr 6, 2026 (91 FR 17327), and issued a Final Decision and compulsory recall order on Apr 29, 2026, banning import and sale of the inflators. It was the agency's first compulsory defect recall order in more than two decades. But NHTSA's own FAQ concedes that "because these inflators were likely illegally imported and a list of affected vehicles is not available, a traditional recall is unlikely." Administrator Jonathan Morrison told the Wall Street Journal the same: there are no records of who imported or distributed the parts.

Contrast that with a genuine manufacturer campaign — for example a Takata-era airbag recall, where you genuinely can look up how to check if a used car has an open airbag recall by VIN, or the Honda Odyssey inflator campaign where owners can confirm how to check if a used honda odyssey has an open airbag recall. Those attach to the manufacturer and the VIN. A counterfeit part does not.

What is a DTN60DB inflator, and which cars are affected?

The DTN60DB is a frontal driver airbag inflator manufactured in China in 2021-2022 and marked "DTN60DB" on the inflator cap. NHTSA named Chevrolet Malibu (2018-2022) and Hyundai Sonata (2017-2019) in its initial decision, but it explicitly warns the problem is not confirmed to be limited to those models — and the August 2026 Equinox death proved the point. There are no affected trims, because the defect is the replacement part, not the factory build. The same open question applies across brands: even a model with its own separate airbag concern, such as when buyers research used land rover defender problems to check before buying, a counterfeit replacement part would sit outside every one of those manufacturer campaigns.

What a VIN check can and can't tell you here

A VIN check cannot prove whether an airbag is genuine or counterfeit — no VIN tool can, because there is no VIN-to-part linkage to look up. What a VIN report can do is surface the accident and salvage history that tells you an inspection is warranted. Use the recall screen for what it is (a presence check), and lean on history to catch the mismatch.

The value here is the corroborating history that justifies the inspection.

Frequently asked questions

How do I check if a used car has a fake or aftermarket airbag? No VIN lookup can confirm it. Cross-reference recall, accident, airbag-deployment, and salvage-auction history: a prior deployment or salvage record while the seller claims "no accidents" is the signal to demand a physical airbag-module inspection by a certified technician before buying.

Why doesn't a VIN recall check show a counterfeit or aftermarket airbag? A VIN recall search screens for manufacturer recall campaigns tied to factory-installed parts. Counterfeit DTN60DB inflators are installed by third parties after a crash and are not tied to any VIN or campaign, so NHTSA can identify zero affected vehicles by VIN.

Does a clean NHTSA recall result mean the airbag is genuine and will deploy? No. A "no open recalls" result only means no manufacturer campaign is open for that VIN. It is not evidence the airbag is a genuine OEM part or that it will deploy safely. Only a physical inspection can confirm that.

Which cars are affected by the DTN60DB airbag inflator? NHTSA named Chevrolet Malibu (2018-2022) and Hyundai Sonata (2017-2019), but warns the problem is not limited to them. An Aug 27, 2026 death in a 2018 Chevrolet Equinox was the first outside that pattern. The defect is a replacement part, not a factory build.

What should I do if the VIN shows a prior airbag deployment but the seller says "no accidents"? Treat the mismatch as a red flag. A car whose airbag deployed had the module replaced by someone; if that person used a counterfeit inflator, it will not show in any recall search. Require a pre-purchase inspection of the airbag module before you buy.

How much does a counterfeit airbag cost versus a genuine one? Counterfeit inflators have sold for roughly $100 on marketplaces like eBay and Facebook, versus about ten times that for an OEM part, per Wall Street Journal reporting. That price gap is why rebuilders installed them in salvage-repaired cars.

What do I do if I think my car has a DTN60DB inflator? Do not rely on a VIN search. Contact NHTSA (1-888-327-4236) or a franchised dealer and arrange a physical inspection of the driver airbag module by a certified technician — the only way NHTSA says the part can be confirmed.

Before you buy
