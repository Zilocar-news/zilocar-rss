What Is a Salvage Title? What It Means Before You Buy a Used Car (2026)

A salvage title is the brand a state applies after an insurer declares a car a total loss — the repair cost plus salvage value would top the car's pre-damage market value. A salvage car is not roadworthy and cannot be registered or driven until repaired and re-inspected, at which point it becomes "rebuilt," never "clean." A VIN check surfaces the salvage or junk auction record; NMVTIS and your state DMV hold the legal brand.

Key facts

  • Definition (NMVTIS/DOJ): a salvage automobile is one damaged so that "its fair salvage value plus the cost of repairing the automobile for legal operation on public streets, roads, and highways would be more than the fair market value of the automobile immediately before the event that caused the damage."
  • What triggers it: a total-loss declaration by the insurer. States use either a fixed percentage of Actual Cash Value (ACV) or the Total Loss Formula (TLF): repair cost + salvage value > ACV.
  • Threshold range (2026): roughly 60% to 100% of ACV across states. Lowest fixed percentage is Oklahoma at 60%; the highest fixed threshold is 100% (Alaska, Arizona, Colorado, Connecticut, Idaho, Texas). Source: WalletHub 2026 guide, cross-checked against CarInsurance.com, Policygenius and SoFi.
  • The ladder: Clean -> Salvage (totaled, not drivable) -> Rebuilt/Reconstructed (repaired, re-inspected, registrable, permanently branded). Junk is terminal.
  • Permanence: once a state applies a brand, "that brand becomes a permanent part of the vehicle's NMVTIS record." There is no path back to clean.
  • Title washing: retitling across state lines to strip a brand is a documented fraud risk, not legal and not universal.
  • Figures are third-party and reflect sources as of 2026-09-17; classification for several states is contested (see below) and should be re-verified against your state DMV.

What is a salvage title, in plain English?

A salvage title means an insurance company looked at a damaged car, added up what it would cost to make it road-legal again plus what the wreck is worth as salvage, and found that total exceeded the car's pre-damage value. At that point the insurer declares it a total loss, and the state issues a salvage brand. A car with a salvage title cannot legally be registered or driven on public roads in that condition.

The damage does not have to be a collision. NMVTIS lists "collision, fire, flood, accident, trespass, or other event" as triggers, so a flood-soaked car or a fire-damaged one can carry the same brand. That is why salvage and flood histories often travel together, and why buyers checking for one should check if a used car has flood or salvage history as a single question.

What percentage of a car's value triggers a salvage title in my state?

There is no single national number. States use one of two mechanisms: a fixed percentage of Actual Cash Value (ACV), or the Total Loss Formula (TLF), under which a car is totaled when repair cost plus salvage value exceeds ACV. What is the difference between a salvage title and a rebuilt title?

A salvage title marks a car that has been totaled and is not roadworthy. A rebuilt (or "reconstructed") title is what a state issues after that salvage car is repaired and passes a state salvage or anti-theft inspection, making it registrable and drivable again. The two are sequential stages, not synonyms: a repaired salvage car becomes rebuilt. For the full mechanics of that repaired-and-re-inspected stage, see what is a rebuilt title.

"Rebuilt" is a state-level term with no federal NMVTIS definition, so the exact label, inspection requirement and wording differ by state ("reconstructed," "prior salvage," "salvage-rebuilt"). What is consistent everywhere is the boundary: a repaired salvage car never becomes clean. Junk sits below salvage as the most severe brand — one item on the wider list of title brands a state can stamp on a vehicle.

A rebuilt-title car is legal to insure, finance and drive, but the brand follows it for life and dents its resale value. Industry guides such as Edmunds and Kelley Blue Book describe steep discounts versus a clean-title equivalent; the exact figure varies by car, condition and market and is not a fixed number, so treat any single percentage you see as an estimate rather than a rule.

Can a salvage title ever become a clean title again?

No. There is no legal route from salvage back to clean. The best outcome a totaled car can reach is a rebuilt or reconstructed title after repair and re-inspection, and that brand is permanent. NMVTIS states plainly that once a titling agency applies a brand, "that brand becomes a permanent part of the vehicle's NMVTIS record."

This matters because "cleared" is not a legal title status. A seller who says a salvage car has been "cleared" is describing a rebuilt car, not a clean one. If you are trying to confirm the current status yourself, the reliable move is to verify a used car title is clean before buying through the state DMV rather than trusting a paper title at face value.

What is title washing, and how does a salvage car end up with a clean-looking title?

Title washing is the practice of retitling a vehicle across state lines specifically to strip an existing brand, exploiting states with weaker brand-carryover rules so a fresh paper title comes out looking clean. It is a real, documented fraud risk — not universal, and not legal. It is exactly the loophole NMVTIS was built to close, by making the brand a permanent VIN-keyed record regardless of what a subsequent paper title shows.

The tell that survives a washed title is the paper trail underneath it. A car that was totaled almost always passed through a salvage or junk auction (Copart- or IAA-style) on its way from the insurer to a rebuilder. That auction record is tied to the VIN and does not disappear when the title is re-issued in a friendlier state, which is why an independent VIN history is a useful cross-check against the paper title in your hand.

How do I check for salvage or rebuilt history by VIN?

Go to the authorities first. For the legal title brand, the state DMV and an NMVTIS-approved provider are definitive; NMVTIS consumer reports run through DOJ-approved providers and cost roughly $3.50 to $14.99 per VIN, varying by provider. For a free insurer-based screen, NICB VINCheck (nicb.org/vincheck) covers salvage, flood and theft records reported by member insurers — though NICB explicitly warns it is "not a comprehensive vehicle history report."

A paid vehicle history report adds the layer those tools miss: the salvage or junk auction record tied to the VIN, alongside accident, odometer, ownership and sales-listing history. A Zilocar VIN check is one such option — it surfaces the total-loss and salvage-auction footprint that survives a washed title, but it does not read state title records and cannot state the legal brand itself. Use it alongside the DMV and NMVTIS, not instead of them.

Who should use which

  • Confirming the legal title brand: the state DMV and an NMVTIS-approved provider. Nothing else is authoritative.
  • A free first screen: NICB VINCheck, understanding its member-insurer-only limits.
  • Full history before you pay: a paid report (Carfax, AutoCheck, Bumper or Zilocar) for accident, odometer, ownership, salvage-auction and — where offered — sales-listing history.
  • One-off vs many cars: a single report suits a one-time buyer; AutoCheck's 25-report pack or a monthly plan suits shoppers comparing many cars.

What to verify by VIN before you pay

  1. Salvage or junk auction record — the footprint that survives a washed title.
  2. Accident and airbag-deployment history — severity and location of any prior damage.
  3. Odometer consistency — rollbacks are common on rebuilt cars.
  4. Theft record — cross-check NICB.
  5. Ownership and title timeline — rapid moves across states are a title-washing red flag.
  6. The legal brand at the DMV/NMVTIS — always confirm the classification with the authority before money changes hands.
