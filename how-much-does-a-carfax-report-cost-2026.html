Is a Carfax report free?

No — there is no way to pull a free Carfax report on any VIN you choose. Carfax's own support center is explicit: you see a free report only when someone else has already paid for that specific vehicle. That means the "free Carfax" you find is always tied to a car that is for sale or one you already own, never an arbitrary VIN you type in.

Three legitimate free routes exist, all confirmed by Carfax's own support pages or corroborating coverage:

  1. Carfax Used Car Listings. Every used car listed for sale on carfax.com comes with a free Carfax Report, because the seller or dealer already paid for it.
  2. Dealer and third-party listings. Dealers who subscribe to Carfax show a "View the free CARFAX Report" link on their own sites and on AutoTrader, Cars.com, and CarGurus listings. If no link appears, Carfax advises asking the dealer or seller directly.
  3. myCarfax / Car Care account. A free account gives you a free Carfax Report on a car you already own — you register your own VIN.

Which report should you use?

Match the route to how many cars you're checking and what data you actually need. If a free report is already attached to the listing, start there — it costs nothing.

  • Checking one car, and it's listed somewhere: use the free route first. If the dealer's listing has no "View free CARFAX Report" link, ask them for one, or check the same car on eBay Motors for a free AutoCheck.
  • Checking one car with no free report attached: a single paid report makes sense. On the single report, AutoCheck is reported cheaper than Carfax ($29.99 vs $44.99); Carfax has the larger dealer/service-record network. Neither is universally "better" — they pull from different data sources.
  • Shopping several cars over a month: a subscription or multi-pack beats single reports. Carfax's $39.99/month unlimited (30-day window) or AutoCheck's 25-pack at $49.99 spread the cost. Zilocar's plan is $29.50 for the first month, then $59.75/month for 20 reports.
  • You only need title-brand, salvage, or odometer flags: an NMVTIS report from an approved provider (VinAudit, ClearVin) is the authoritative source at roughly $2 to $13.
  • You only need open recalls: NHTSA's free lookup is enough — no report purchase required.

What to verify by VIN before you pay for any report

Whether the report is free or paid, these are the concrete items worth confirming on a used car before money changes hands:

  1. Open recalls. Run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls — it's free and authoritative. A history report shows recall presence, but only NHTSA and the dealer can tell you whether a specific recall was actually remedied; what "recall completed" means when buying a used car explains why a stamp isn't proof.
  2. Accident and airbag-deployment history. Look for reported collisions, damage severity, and whether airbags deployed.
  3. Odometer / rollback check. Compare the reading against the mileage recorded across past listings and title events.
  4. Title, salvage, and junk flags. NMVTIS and your state DMV are the authorities on the legal title brand; a history report may surface the salvage or junk auction record that precedes a brand.
  5. Theft record. A stolen-vehicle flag (NICB data) is a hard stop.
  6. Ownership and listing history. How many owners, and how the car has moved through past sales listings and asking prices.

What a VIN check can and can't tell you here

A commercial VIN-history report surfaces what has been reported about a car's past — accidents, ownership, listings — but it cannot confirm mechanical repairs were done correctly, and it is not a substitute for NHTSA's recall data or a state DMV title record. Know the boundary before you rely on any single report.

Take one paid alternative, Zilocar, as a concrete example of where the line falls. A Zilocar VIN check can surface: recall presence and count (equivalent to NHTSA's free lookup — count only); accident and damage records including location, type, severity, and airbag-deployment status; odometer/rollback check; theft records (NICB); junk and salvage auction records; ownership history; sales-listing history (past and current listings, asking prices, mileage, days-on-market); specs and options; NHTSA and IIHS safety ratings; and market valuation.

It cannot: confirm whether a recall was actually remedied or flashed; flag open NHTSA investigations (PE/EA); show per-unit dealer firmware or remedy detail; or show the legal title-brand classification itself — it shows the junk/salvage auction record, not the DMV's brand. For those, cede to NHTSA, the state DMV, and the dealer. The difference between a closed recall and an open probe matters: what "recall closed" means on a VIN check walks through it.

Zilocar is an alternative history report — never a Carfax reprint, resale, or equal, and its data does not come from Carfax.

Frequently asked questions

Is a Carfax report free? Not on demand. You only see a free Carfax when a dealer or seller already paid for that specific car's report — through a Carfax used-car listing, a dealer's website, or listing sites like AutoTrader and Cars.com. There is no way to pull a free Carfax on an arbitrary VIN you choose.

How much does a Carfax report cost in 2026? A single Carfax report is reported at $44.99 as of 2026-09-15. Multi-packs are reported around $79.99 for three (one source records $59.99) and $99.99 for five, plus a $39.99/month unlimited subscription within a 30-day window. Verify each price at Carfax checkout on the day you buy.

How can I get a free Carfax report by VIN? You can't request one for any VIN. Free Carfax reports exist only where someone already paid: cars listed on carfax.com, dealer listings that show a "View free CARFAX Report" link, and vehicles you already own via a free myCarfax/Car Care account. If no link appears, ask the dealer directly.

What's the difference between Carfax and AutoCheck, and which is cheaper? Both are full history reports from different data networks. On the single report, AutoCheck is reported cheaper ($29.99 vs Carfax's $44.99) and is known for its 100-point AutoCheck Score and auction-history strength; Carfax has a larger dealer and service-record network. Cheaper on the single report is AutoCheck; neither is universally more complete.

Is AutoCheck really free on eBay Motors? Yes. eBay Motors provides a free Experian AutoCheck report on used-vehicle listings through an exclusive eBay-Experian arrangement; eBay pays Experian and sellers can't opt out. It's reported to cover vehicles roughly 1981 model-year and newer. Confirm the offer is live before relying on it.

Is a Carfax monthly subscription worth it if I'm shopping for one car? Usually not. The $39.99/month unlimited plan pays off only if you'll check several cars within the 30-day window. For a single car, a one-off report — Carfax, AutoCheck, or an alternative — costs less than committing to a subscription you'd cancel after one lookup.

Is there a free government way to check a car's title, salvage, or odometer? Partly. NHTSA's recall lookup at nhtsa.gov/recalls is free but covers recalls only. For title brand, salvage, junk, and odometer flags, NMVTIS (vehiclehistory.gov) is the federal authority, but its reports are sold through approved providers for roughly $2 to $13 — the .gov portal itself does not give them away.

Are the cheap $5.99 "Carfax reseller" sites legit? Treat them with caution. Many low-cost "Carfax for $4.50" sites are unofficial affiliate operations, and their legitimacy and safety are unverified. For a genuine Carfax at Carfax's price, buy from carfax.com; for a lower-cost history report, use a named alternative like AutoCheck, an NMVTIS provider, or Zilocar.
