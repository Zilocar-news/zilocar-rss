Used EVs Are the Only Cars Getting Pricier: Why Every Tesla Made the Top-10 Gainers List

In June 2026, used electric vehicles were the only segment of a softening used-car market that got more expensive. iSeeCars' July 21 study put the average used EV at $33,305, up 7.4% year over year, while used cars overall fell 1.3% to $32,395. All four Tesla models made the top-10 price gainers list. A VIN check verifies a used EV's accident, salvage, odometer, and recall-presence history, though not battery health.

Key facts

  • Used EV average price: +7.4% year over year to $33,305 in June 2026 (a +$2,308 gain), per iSeeCars.
  • Overall used-car market: moved the opposite way — -1.3% year over year to $32,395 (-$412). Used EVs now sit above the overall used average.
  • Acceleration: the used-EV year-over-year gain rose three straight months — +2.7% (April) → +5.2% (May) → +7.4% (June).
  • All four Teslas were top-10 gainers: Model X +17.5% to $60,950 (#1 overall), Model 3 +13.5% to $28,520 (#5), Model S +13.1% to $52,787 (#7), Model Y +12.0% to $32,916 (#9).
  • Wholesale confirms it: the Manheim Used Vehicle Value Index read 211.5 in mid-July 2026 (-0.6% MoM, +2% YoY); the Manheim EV Index was +12.4% YoY versus just +1.1% for non-EVs, and EVs passed 4% of index units for the first time ever.
  • Recall context: a 2022 Tesla Model 3 carries 17 NHTSA recall campaigns; a 2021 Model Y carries 22 (as of July 28, 2026) — mostly software/ADAS, and cumulative, not proof any one car is unrepaired.
  • Cited cause (analyst opinion, hedged): higher gas prices during the 2026 Iran conflict pushed buyers toward EVs.

Are used Tesla prices going up right now?

Yes. In June 2026, every Tesla model tracked by iSeeCars posted a year-over-year price increase on used lots, and all four landed in the market's top 10 gainers. The Model X led the entire list at +17.5% to an average $60,950 (+$9,066). The Model 3 rose 13.5% to $28,520, the Model S 13.1% to $52,787, and the Model Y 12.0% to $32,916. iSeeCars Executive Analyst Karl Brauer said "Tesla makes up nearly half of the top 10 used models with the highest price increase."

The figures cover 1-to-5-year-old used vehicles in iSeeCars' analysis.

Why are used EV prices rising when used car prices are falling?

Two independent datasets show the same divergence: used EVs are appreciating while the broader used market softens. iSeeCars (retail listings) put used EVs up 7.4% year over year even as the overall used average fell 1.3%. Cox Automotive's Manheim index (wholesale auctions) showed its EV sub-index up 12.4% year over year against just 1.1% for non-EVs. When retail and wholesale move together, the trend is structural rather than a single-source blip.

Analysts attribute the shift to fuel costs. iSeeCars' Karl Brauer ties the demand for used EVs to "rising fuel costs" during the 2026 Iran conflict, adding that "it took several weeks before buyers adjusted." Gas climbed after the February 28, 2026 US/Israel strike on Iran and Strait of Hormuz disruption; CNBC and Cox economists connect the used-EV demand surge to war-driven pump prices, and Autotrader logged used-EV inquiries up 15% in the war's first month. This causation is attributed analyst opinion and is explicitly hedged — it is not an established fact, and it is not a Zilocar finding.

Is now a bad time to buy a used Tesla — am I overpaying?

If you are shopping a 3-to-4-year-old used Tesla or EV in mid-2026, you are buying into a segment near its recent high, so the risk of overpaying is elevated. The defense is the car's own price record: compare today's asking number against the same vehicle's prior listing prices and mileage rather than the seller's headline figure. A car re-listed at a sharp markup over its earlier ask, with no change in mileage or condition, is riding the market spike, not offering value.

Elevated prices also raise the stakes on condition. When you pay a premium, an undisclosed accident, an airbag deployment, an odometer discrepancy, or a salvage-auction record erodes more of your money than it would on a cheaper car. That makes history verification more important at a market top, not less.

How to check a used Tesla or EV by VIN before you overpay

Before paying a 2026 premium, verify the specific car's history and open recalls. Start with the authoritative free tools, then layer in a history report:

  1. NHTSA (free): check recalls by VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls to see whether the car falls within any open recall campaign. This is the primary source for recall data.
  2. Dealer or OEM inspection: the only way to learn an EV's battery state-of-health, state-of-charge, remaining range, and whether a given recall was actually remedied or flashed. Cede these to the shop; no history report can prove them.
  3. A VIN history report (for example, Zilocar): presented alongside NHTSA's free tool, it surfaces the car's sales-listing history — past and current listings, asking prices, mileage, and days-on-market — so you can judge whether today's ask is inflated versus its own record. It also screens for recall presence and reports accident and airbag-deployment records, odometer/rollback checks, junk and salvage-auction records, theft (NICB), ownership count, specs and options, NHTSA and IIHS safety ratings, and market valuation.

What a VIN check can and can't tell you here

A VIN check is a pre-purchase overpayment defense: it verifies a used EV's history and asking-price trail, not its battery. Use it to catch a car whose price, accident record, or salvage history should give you pause — and defer battery health and remedy confirmation to a dealer or OEM inspection.

On recalls specifically: a used Tesla carries a heavy campaign footprint — 17 for a 2022 Model 3, 22 for a 2021 Model Y, 19 for a 2022 Model Y, and 16 for a 2023 Model Y as of July 28, 2026. Most are software or ADAS items (for example, FSD campaigns 22V037000 and 23V838000, and TPMS campaign 24V935000 covering 2017-2025 Model 3s). The newest surfaced is 26V283000 (a backover/software fix for 2017-2023 Model 3 and 2020-2023 Model Y). These are cumulative counts of every campaign ever issued for the model year, and most are over-the-air remedied — they show why recall screening matters when you overpay, not that any specific VIN is unrepaired.
