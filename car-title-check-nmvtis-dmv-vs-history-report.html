Car Title Check: NMVTIS, the DMV, and What a History Report Actually Proves

A title check has one authority for the legal brand. The state DMV applies and prints the title brand — Salvage, Rebuilt, Junk, Flood, Lemon — and NMVTIS, the federal National Motor Vehicle Title Information System at vehiclehistory.gov, carries that brand from every state. A commercial vehicle history report does not return the legal brand; for consumers, Carfax and AutoCheck are not even approved NMVTIS providers. It shows title and ownership history plus the junk/salvage auction record — an earlier, different signal — not the brand itself.

Title check: the key facts

  • Authority for the legal brand: the issuing state DMV sets it; NMVTIS aggregates it. AAMVA calls NMVTIS "the only vehicle history database in the nation" that every state motor-vehicle agency, insurer and junk/salvage operator must report to by federal law, and NMVTIS "is required to include data from all state motor vehicle agencies", data that AAMVA describes as covering "brand information that describes a vehicle's prior use and condition and the latest odometer reading."
  • A consumer Carfax or AutoCheck report is not an NMVTIS report. The DOJ/BJA provider list places Carfax, DMVdesk and Experian in the "commercial customers only" group and states that individual consumers cannot get NMVTIS reports from them.
  • An NMVTIS report contains: current and prior titles, the latest odometer reading recorded at titling, brand history, total-loss records (from insurers) and salvage records (from auctions, recyclers, rebuilders). It does not contain owner names, minor accident records, repair histories or recall data.
  • Approved consumer providers: roughly 12 public providers are listed at vehiclehistory.gov as of 2026-11-04. NMVTIS sets no price; providers do. The lowest single-report price confirmed at the provider on 2026-11-04 is $9.98 (Titlecheck.us); other approved providers report similar single-report prices, and multi-report packs cut the per-report cost to about $3 to $4 (VinAudit, 10 reports for $29.99).
  • Free state checks exist but are limited: New York's online Title Status and Florida's Motor Vehicle Information Check both return limited data at $0 and do not return full brand history.

Who is the authoritative source — the DMV, NMVTIS, or a history report?

The issuing state DMV creates the legal title brand, and NMVTIS is the authoritative federal feed that carries it across state lines. A commercial history report is a useful but separate product: it compiles title, ownership and some auction data and adds accidents, service and recalls — but it is not the legal brand. Does a Carfax or AutoCheck report show the official title brand?

No — not as the federal, legally recorded brand. The legal brand originates at the state DMV and is recorded in NMVTIS; a commercial report shows that vendor's own compiled title data. Carfax, DMVdesk and Experian appear on the DOJ/BJA list only as commercial providers serving dealerships (Carfax commercial use is noted as California-only), and the list states individual consumers cannot get NMVTIS reports from them. A consumer Carfax or AutoCheck report may be excellent for accidents and service history, but it is not the NMVTIS brand feed — our guide to what does carfax show covers what it does and doesn't include. For the brand of record, go to NMVTIS or the issuing DMV instead.

What is the difference between NMVTIS and a commercial history report?

NMVTIS is the one database all states, insurers and salvage operators must report to, so it is the single place a state-applied brand is guaranteed to surface; a commercial report is privately compiled and adds data NMVTIS omits. An NMVTIS report is title-, brand-, odometer-, total-loss- and salvage-centric and carries no accident severity, repair history or recall data; a commercial report fills those gaps. That is why many buyers run both — NMVTIS or the DMV for the brand of record, a history report for accidents and service. Where can I get an official NMVTIS report, and how much does it cost?

You get an NMVTIS report from one of the approved public providers listed at vehiclehistory.gov — about 12 consumer-facing providers as of 2026-11-04 — and the price is set by each provider, not by NMVTIS. The system is funded by user fees, so, as DC DMV puts it quoting NMVTIS, the "cost for NMVTIS vehicle history reports vary depending on the selected data provider." Note that "$0" is not an NMVTIS price — only limited state status checks are free. How do I check a car title for free at the state DMV?

Go to your state DMV's online lookup — the free, authoritative starting point, though it returns less than a full NMVTIS report. In New York, the free nys title check at the DMV's Title Status tool takes a VIN, model year and make and returns the title issue date, number of liens and the lienholder's name and address, with no owner info; a certified abstract (Form MV-15) is ordered by mail. In Florida, the free florida title check through FLHSMV's Motor Vehicle Information Check takes a VIN or title number and returns limited information, with a formal record via Form HSMV 90510. Neither returns full brand history — for that, use an NMVTIS report or a certified abstract. (Third-party fee figures, such as a $7 NY abstract fee, are not confirmed on an official DMV schedule.)

What is a salvage title vs rebuilt vs junk vs branded title?

These are the brands a state DMV can apply, and each changes what you can register, insure and safely pay. In short: salvage means the vehicle was declared a total loss; rebuilt means a salvage vehicle was repaired and passed a state inspection; junk means not roadworthy, possibly parts-only; "branded title" is the umbrella for any non-clean brand. This hub routes to the dedicated guides rather than re-explain each: what is a salvage title, what is a rebuilt title, the side-by-side rebuilt vs salvage title, and what is a branded title. Florida shows how literal the brand is: under Chapter 319, a rebuildable total loss gets a "Salvage Rebuildable" title that becomes "Rebuilt" after inspection, and removing a rebuilt decal to conceal status is a third-degree felony.

What a VIN check can and can't tell you here

A VIN check is the fast, cheap early screen; it catches the signals a brand would be built on, but it is not the legal word on the brand. Run by VIN, a Zilocar check surfaces title and ownership history, junk and salvage auction records, accident and damage records (location, type, severity, airbag deployment), an odometer/rollback check, theft records (NICB), sales-listing history (prices, mileage, days on market), specs, NHTSA and IIHS safety ratings, market valuation, and recall presence/count — and flags salvage and rebuilt brand signals on its sample report.

What it does not do: it does not show the official title certificate or the legal title-brand classification, does not confirm whether a recall was remedied (presence/count only), and does not track NHTSA investigations or dealer firmware status. The honest workflow is to run the VIN check to catch salvage, accident, odometer and listing red flags cheaply — for example whether a car shows flood or salvage history — then confirm the legal brand at NMVTIS or the DMV before money changes hands.

What to verify by VIN before you pay

  1. Brand signals and auction records — any salvage, junk or total-loss auction activity? If yes, pull the NMVTIS or DMV brand of record.
  2. Odometer consistency — do readings climb in order, with no gaps or rollbacks?
  3. Title and ownership history — how many owners and states, and do the title dates fit the sale story?
  4. Accident and airbag records — any logged collision, severity or airbag deployment?
  5. Liens — any open lienholder on the DMV record that must be paid before clear title transfers?
  6. The brand of record — before you pay, confirm the legal brand at NMVTIS (an approved provider) or the issuing DMV. The VIN check narrows the risk; these two settle it.

Frequently asked questions

Does a Carfax or AutoCheck report show the official title brand? No. The legal brand is set by the state DMV and carried in NMVTIS. Carfax and AutoCheck are not approved NMVTIS providers for consumers, so a consumer report from either is vendor-compiled title data, not the federal brand feed.

Is NMVTIS the same as the DMV? No. The state DMV issues the title and applies the brand; NMVTIS is the federal database every DMV, insurer and salvage yard must report to, aggregating brands from all states into one record a consumer can buy through an approved provider.

Can I get an NMVTIS report from Carfax? Not as a consumer. The DOJ/BJA list places Carfax, DMVdesk and Experian in the commercial-customers-only group. Consumers buy NMVTIS reports from the roughly 12 approved public providers listed at vehiclehistory.gov.

How much does an NMVTIS report cost? NMVTIS sets no price; each provider does. The lowest single-report price we confirmed at the provider on 2026-11-04 is $9.98 at Titlecheck.us; other approved providers are reported near $10, and multi-report packs cut the per-report cost to about $3 to $4.

How do I check a car title for free? Some state DMVs offer a free status check — New York's Title Status tool and Florida's Motor Vehicle Information Check both return limited data at no cost. Neither returns full brand history; for that you need an NMVTIS report or a certified DMV abstract.

Can a VIN check tell me if a car has a clean title? A VIN check surfaces salvage and junk auction records, title and ownership history, and odometer red flags — a strong early screen. It does not return the legal brand classification itself; confirm that at NMVTIS or the issuing DMV before you pay.

Is the title brand permanent, and can it be removed? Brands generally stay on the title record for life and follow the car across states through NMVTIS. A salvage brand can become rebuilt after a state inspection, but the history remains. In Florida, removing a rebuilt decal to hide the status is a third-degree felony.
