Storm-Flooded Cars Are Back With 'Clean' Titles: The Gap a Carfax Report Can Hide Before You Buy

TL;DR: Storm-flooded vehicles are being re-titled in states with weaker checks, a practice called title washing, so the flood or salvage brand disappears and a later history report can read "clean." CARFAX estimated roughly 482,000 water-damaged vehicles were on U.S. roads by early 2025, Florida leading at about 82,000. A VIN check can surface salvage-auction, accident and odometer records that contradict a clean claim, but it cannot read the legal title brand itself.

Key facts

  • Title washing = moving a flood/salvage/total-loss vehicle to a state with weaker retitling checks (or using forged/duplicate titles) so the brand disappears from the paperwork a report queries. It is illegal in every state and can be a felony.
  • ~482,000 water/flood-damaged vehicles were already on U.S. roads at the start of 2025 (CARFAX, Aug 6, 2025).
  • Up to 45,000 more vehicles were flood-damaged in mid-2025 storms across Texas, Kentucky and West Virginia (CARFAX, Aug 6, 2025).
  • ~347,000 vehicles were flood-damaged in calendar 2024; Hurricanes Helene and Milton alone accounted for 250,000-plus (CARFAX, via DCReport, Jan 30, 2026).
  • ~82,000 flood-branded vehicles are on Florida roads, the most of any state; washed cars are excluded from that count (Live Insurance News, May 10, 2026).
  • ~50% of hurricane-damaged vehicles eventually return to market (Florida Automobile Dealers Association, via DCReport).
  • Washing a flood brand can raise a car's asking price 20% to 50% (Dirt Legal).
  • No new 2026 storm-season flood count exists yet. Current totals are accumulated 2024–2025 losses still moving through the used market.
  • This is a consumer-protection explainer, not a recall. There is no NHTSA campaign tied to it.

What is title washing, and how does it erase a flood or salvage brand?

Title washing is the practice of moving a vehicle branded flood, salvage or total-loss to a state with weaker retitling checks so the brand drops off, or using forged and duplicate titles to the same effect. Once the brand is gone from the paperwork, a subsequent history report can read "clean" even though the car was underwater. It is illegal in every state and can be a felony, but enforcement gaps make it common.

The authoritative layer that is supposed to stop this is NMVTIS, the National Motor Vehicle Title Information System, run by the U.S. Department of Justice's Bureau of Justice Assistance. NMVTIS ties state DMV records together so a brand applied in one state should follow the VIN everywhere. Washing works when a retitling state does not consistently check NMVTIS before issuing a fresh title. DCReport (Jan 30, 2026) named California, Idaho, Oklahoma, North Carolina, Maryland and Massachusetts as states that historically lacked consistent NMVTIS checks at retitling. Treat that list as reported rather than as current legal fact, because NMVTIS participation changes over time. Cleaning a title can take "weeks, maybe a month."

Why can a car have a "clean" Carfax or clean title but still have been underwater?

A history report or a paper title reads only the records it can see. If a flood brand was washed off in a weak-enforcement state, or the vehicle's loss was never reported to a source the report queries, the report can show "clean" while the car was submerged. The clean status describes the paperwork, not the metal.

This is why single-source verification fails on flood cars. Florida's widely cited ~82,000 figure counts only vehicles that still carry a flood brand on their title. The successfully washed cars are excluded from that number entirely, and they are the unquantified gap this story turns on. A clean report is not proof a car is dry; it is the absence of a record, which is not the same thing.

How much money is behind this, and where are the cars?

The financial incentive is large and direct. DCReport cites a flood 2020 Toyota Camry bought for $8,000 at a salvage auction and resold for about $22,000; more broadly, washing a flood-branded title can lift an asking price 20% to 50% (Dirt Legal). With the Florida Automobile Dealers Association estimating roughly half of hurricane-damaged vehicles return to market, the volume flowing back to buyers is substantial.

The cars do not stay near the storms. As CARFAX VP Faisal Hasan put it, "Flood-damaged cars don't just stay in storm-hit areas; they show up all over the country," frequently surfacing in non-storm states such as Pennsylvania and Illinois. It can take months to over a year for a flooded car to reach a lot, often after a retitling move, which is exactly why an out-of-state title move from Florida or Texas is worth scrutinizing.

How to check if a used car has flood or salvage history before you buy

Use several sources in combination, because each one covers a different gap. Start with the authoritative free layers, add a full VIN history report, and finish with human eyes on the car. No single tool is sufficient for a flood car.

  1. Pull the free NMVTIS report at the Bureau of Justice Assistance's consumer portal. NMVTIS is the title-history layer that ties state DMV records together, and it is the closest thing to an authoritative title-brand check.
  2. Run NICB VINCheck, which is free and shows whether a VIN was reported salvage, total-loss or stolen-and-unrecovered by participating insurers.
  3. Run a full VIN history report for accident and damage records, salvage and junk auction records, odometer readings, ownership and geographic history, and sales-listing history. A Zilocar VIN check is one option here alongside the authoritative sources above; it surfaces salvage and junk-auction records, accident history with airbag-deployment status, odometer/rollback signals, theft data, ownership history, and past and current sales listings with prices, mileage and days-on-market, which can expose a suspiciously fast cross-state flip.
  4. Use CARFAX Flood Check (carfax.com/flood), a free flood-specific lookup.
  5. Pay for an in-person pre-purchase inspection. A mechanic checks what no database can: mold, silt, corrosion and wiring damage.

Watch for these physical red flags yourself: a musty or moldy odor; damp or mismatched carpet; rust on screws, brackets, door hinges and pedals; silt or mud in the trunk well, under seats or in the glovebox; water lines in the engine bay or lights; fogging inside lamps and the instrument cluster; and brittle or newly replaced wiring.

What a VIN check can and can't tell you here

A VIN history report is powerful for contradicting a "clean" claim, but it has hard limits on this specific story. Its value is surfacing the record layers a washed title tries to bury, not classifying the title brand. Read the split below before you rely on any single report.

Frequently asked questions

How do I check if a used car has flood or salvage history before I buy? Use multiple sources: the free NMVTIS report for title-brand history, the free NICB VINCheck for salvage and total-loss records, and a full VIN history report for accident, auction, odometer and ownership records. Then pay a mechanic for an in-person inspection. No single tool is enough.

Why can a car have a clean Carfax or clean title but still have been underwater? Title washing moves a branded vehicle to a state with weaker retitling checks so the flood or salvage brand drops off, and forged or duplicate titles are also used. A subsequent history report can then read clean because the brand is gone from the paperwork it queries.

What is NMVTIS and how is it different from a Carfax report? NMVTIS, run by the U.S. Bureau of Justice Assistance, ties state DMV title records together and is the authoritative title-history layer. Commercial reports like Carfax draw on their own data sources. Cross-checking both closes gaps neither catches alone.

Is NICB VINCheck free, and what does it miss? Yes, NICB VINCheck is free. It shows whether a VIN was reported salvage, total-loss or stolen-and-unrecovered by participating insurers covering about 88% of the personal-auto market. It does not query non-participating insurers or law enforcement, and total loss does not always mean salvage.

What are the physical warning signs of a flood-damaged car? A musty or moldy smell, damp or mismatched carpet, rust on screws, brackets, door hinges and pedals, silt or mud in the trunk well and under seats, water lines in the engine bay or lights, fogging inside lamps and the instrument cluster, and brittle or newly replaced wiring.

Can a flood car show up for sale far from where the storm hit? Yes. CARFAX reports flood cars surface nationwide, including non-storm states like Pennsylvania and Illinois. It can take months to over a year for flooded vehicles to reach the market, often after being retitled in another state.

Can a VIN check tell me whether airbags deployed or the odometer was rolled back? A history report can surface accident and damage records including airbag-deployment status, plus odometer readings that flag possible rollback. It cannot read the legal title brand itself, confirm a recall was repaired, or track open safety investigations.
