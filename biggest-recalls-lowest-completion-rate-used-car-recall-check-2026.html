The Biggest Recalls Are the Least Likely to Be Fixed — What NHTSA Completion Data Says Before You Buy

TL;DR: Across 10,494 NHTSA vehicle recall campaigns (frozen file, retrieved 2026-08-12), completion falls as recall size rises: campaigns over 1,000,000 vehicles average 63.5% completion (median 67.7%) versus 72.8% for recalls under 100,000. The gap widens on the fairer 2020-2024 cohort. So the biggest, most-publicized recalls a used buyer meets are the least likely to already be fixed, and a VIN check flags exposure, not repair.

Key facts

  • Finding: Larger recalls are repaired less often. Under 100,000 units: 72.8% mean / 81.9% median completion. Over 1,000,000 units: 63.5% mean / 67.7% median.
  • Robustness: On the 2020-2024 cohort (old enough to be fixed), the small-vs-mega mean gap grows from ~9.3 to ~10.7 points, so the pattern survives the "too new to be fixed" objection.
  • Dataset: N = 10,494 NHTSA vehicle recall campaigns; report dates 2011-05-25 to 2026-06-15; retrieved 2026-08-12.
  • News peg 1 — Ram 1500 (26V495000): 1,271,294 trucks, MY2019-2026; second-row driver-side and center seat-belt buckle anchors may not be secured to the body; dealer reattaches, free; owner letters began ~Aug 18, 2026; no injuries reported.
  • News peg 2 — Toyota Camry Hybrid (26V511000): ~508,354 vehicles, MY2025-2026 with the 7.0-inch cluster; software fault can leave the display blank at startup (no speedometer, fuel gauge, warnings) and disable turn/hazard signals; dealer software update, free, not over-the-air.
  • Neither news-peg campaign is in the frozen file (it ends 2026-06-15), so both have effectively zero measured completion — they are entering the used pipeline before any repairs have accrued.

Do dealers have to fix recalls before selling a used car?

No federal law requires it. Franchised new-car dealers are barred from selling a new vehicle with an open recall, but there is no equivalent federal rule for used cars — independent lots and private sellers can legally sell a used car with an unrepaired safety recall. That means the responsibility to confirm the repair falls on the buyer, not the seller. Because the free remedy transfers with the car, you can still get it fixed after purchase, but you should never assume it was already done.

This is exactly where recall completion data matters. A recall being issued is not the same as a recall being repaired, and the gap between the two is large and predictable by size.

Does recall size predict whether it gets fixed? Yes — bigger means less-fixed.

Recall size predicts completion, and the relationship runs the opposite way to intuition: the bigger the recall, the smaller the share of vehicles actually repaired. Across all 10,494 vehicle campaigns in our file, median completion falls monotonically from 81.9% for sub-100,000 recalls to 67.7% for recalls over a million units. The all-years means decline as a clear gradient (one adjacent pair, 250k-500k vs 500k-1M, is effectively tied within a tenth of a point), and on the 2020-2024 cohort the means are strictly monotonic.

The most common objection is that big recalls simply need more time. We tested that directly: restricting to campaigns reported in 2020-2024 — old enough that repairs should have landed — does not close the gap, it widens it. The mean spread between the smallest and largest bins grows from about 9.3 points (all years) to about 10.7 points (2020-2024 cohort). Whatever drives low completion on mega-recalls — scale, parts backlogs, resold cars, owners who moved — is structural, not a timing artifact. Which real mega-recalls are the least-completed?

The clearest examples are the airbag and inflator campaigns, and several sit far below even the 1M+ average. The single largest campaign in the file, Honda's 6,281,043-unit driver frontal airbag recall (15V320000), reached 79.0% — but that is the exception. GM's 5,877,718-unit ignition-switch recall (14V400000) sits at 37.1%, and Takata-related "Do Not Drive" and inflator campaigns cluster in the 35-47% band, with one Honda inflator recall (20V026000, 1,439,841 units) at just 7.9%.

Why the fresh Ram 1500 and Camry Hybrid recalls are the ones to verify

Both new campaigns are large, recent, and — critically — not yet in any completion dataset, which is precisely why a buyer should treat them as unresolved. The Ram 1500 recall (26V495000) covers 1,271,294 trucks from model years 2019-2026: at the factory, the second-row driver-side and center seat-belt buckle anchors may not have been properly secured to the body structure, so a belt might not restrain an occupant in a crash. Stellantis opened its internal investigation on Feb 26, 2026 after a Transport Canada customer-concern report; no injuries have been reported. The dealer inspects and, if needed, reattaches the anchors free of charge, with owner letters starting around Aug 18, 2026.

The Camry Hybrid recall (26V511000) covers about 508,354 MY2025-2026 cars fitted with the 7.0-inch combination meter (higher trims with the 12.3-inch display are excluded). A software fault can leave that cluster blank at startup — no speedometer, fuel gauge, or warning lamps — and can also deactivate the exterior turn signals, hazard lamps, seat-belt warning, and smart-key reminder, which fails Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards. The remedy is a dealer software update: free, but not over-the-air, so the car must physically visit a dealer. (The Camry campaign number is news-verified and awaiting direct nhtsa.gov confirmation, and reported owner-notification dates for it vary, so confirm your VIN with Toyota or NHTSA rather than a headline date.)

Because both recalls are days old, huge numbers of these vehicles will change hands before repairs are logged. That is the entire lesson of the completion data in miniature: the freshest, biggest, most-publicized recall is the one least likely to already be handled on the specific car in front of you.

Is it safe to buy a used car with an open recall?

It can be safe once the free remedy is completed, but an open safety recall means a known defect is currently unrepaired on that vehicle. Treat any flagged recall as unresolved until you have proof otherwise: a repair-order line item, a dealer confirmation, or a clean result from NHTSA's VIN tool. The repair is free to you as the new owner, so an open recall is a reason to schedule the fix, not necessarily to walk away — but it is a hard reason to verify before you hand over money. What a VIN check can and can't tell you here

A VIN check tells you a car's recall exposure — that it falls within one or more campaigns — but not the remedy status. This distinction is the whole point of this story: because mega-recalls are the least-completed, a flagged recall must be treated as potentially open until confirmed. Use the free tool at nhtsa.gov/recalls first, and the fastest way to learn how to check a used car for open recalls before buying is to run the VIN there and then call a franchised dealer of that brand for the repair-order history.

Frequently asked questions

Do dealers have to fix recalls before selling a used car? No. Federal law bars franchised new-car dealers from selling a new car under an open recall, but there is no equivalent federal rule for used cars. Independent lots and private sellers can legally sell a used car with an unrepaired safety recall, so verify the remedy yourself.

Does a VIN check show whether a recall was actually fixed? Not by itself. A VIN recall screen surfaces presence and count — that a car falls within one or more campaigns — the same exposure signal NHTSA's free tool gives. It does not track open-versus-closed or remedied status. Confirm the repair with the dealer or NHTSA's VIN lookup.

Why would a huge, well-publicized recall still be unrepaired? Our tabulation of 10,494 NHTSA vehicle campaigns shows completion falls as recall size rises: over 1,000,000 vehicles it averages 63.5% (median 67.7%) versus 72.8% for sub-100,000 recalls. Scale, parts backlogs, resold cars, and lost owners all leave big recalls least-completed.

Is my 2019-2026 Ram 1500 in the seat-belt recall, and does it cost anything? Campaign 26V495000 covers 1,271,294 Ram 1500 trucks whose second-row driver-side and center seat-belt buckle anchors may not be properly secured. The dealer inspects and reattaches them free. Owner notification began around August 18, 2026. Verify your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls.

Is the 2025-2026 Camry Hybrid cluster recall just a software update? Per campaign 26V511000, about 508,354 Camry Hybrids with the 7.0-inch cluster can boot to a blank display, losing the speedometer and gauges. The fix is a dealer software update — free, but not over-the-air — so it requires a physical visit. Confirm eligibility with Toyota or NHTSA.

Who pays for a recall repair on a used car? The manufacturer. Safety-recall repairs are free to the current owner regardless of how many times the car has been resold, as long as it is within the covered population. You do not have to be the original buyer.

Can I still register or drive a used car with an unrepaired recall? In most of the US you can still register and drive it, but a Do Not Drive recall is an explicit instruction to stop until repaired. Whatever the class, an open recall is unresolved risk — schedule the free fix as soon as possible.
