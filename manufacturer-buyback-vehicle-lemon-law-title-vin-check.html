What Is a Manufacturer Buyback Vehicle? Lemon-Law Buyback Titles and How to Check One by VIN

A manufacturer buyback — also called a lemon-law buyback — is a vehicle the automaker repurchased because a warranty defect could not be fixed within the limit a state lemon law allows. It is a defect brand, not a crash brand: unlike salvage, it implies no collision, flood, or theft damage. In most states the title is re-branded and the defect must be disclosed before resale.

What is a manufacturer buyback vehicle?

A manufacturer buyback vehicle is a car the automaker repurchased from its owner because a warranty defect — legally a "nonconformity" — could not be fixed within the allowance a state lemon law sets. That allowance is generally a defined number of repair attempts for the same problem, or a defined number of cumulative days out of service. When the maker buys the car back, most states require the title to be re-branded so the defect history travels with the vehicle.

The federal Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act gives warranty remedies but does not set repurchase thresholds; those live in each state's lemon statute, so the exact trigger differs from state to state. This brand sits in the same branded title family states use to flag a vehicle's history, but it is the only common brand that points to a defect rather than damage.

Key facts at a glance:

  • Federal layer: the FTC Used Car Rule (16 CFR Part 455) requires a Buyers Guide on every used car, but it has no buyback or lemon-law disclosure box and defers to the state attorney general. There is no federal lemon-law buyback mandate; thresholds are set by state law.
  • Brand authority: NMVTIS, run by the U.S. Department of Justice, Bureau of Justice Assistance, keeps the history of title brands applied by any state, so a buyback/lemon brand follows the VIN across state lines.
  • California example (verified 2026-11-02 — the brand and the signed disclosure at Cal. Civ. Code §1793.23, the date cutoff and the decal location at DMV VIRPM §2.040, which implements Veh. Code §§4453 and 11713.12): vehicles reacquired for a warranty defect on or after January 1, 1996 are branded "LEMON LAW BUYBACK"; those reacquired on or before December 31, 1995 are branded "WARRANTY RETURN" (some registration certificates show the abbreviated "WARNTY RET"). A decal on the left-door frame and a signed pre-sale disclosure are both required.
  • Thresholds and even the brand name vary by state (Florida uses "Manufacturer Buyback"); do not assume one state's rule applies in another.

Is a manufacturer buyback the same as a salvage or rebuilt title?

No. A buyback or lemon-law brand is a warranty/defect brand and does not imply the car was crashed, flooded, burned, or stolen. A salvage title means an insurer declared the vehicle a total loss, and a rebuilt title means a salvage car was repaired and re-certified for the road. Those are damage brands; a buyback is not.

That distinction matters when you weigh rebuilt vs salvage title risk: there is no wreck in the car's past, only an unresolved defect. The catch is the mirror image — with salvage you can often inspect the repaired damage, while a buyback's defect may be intermittent electronics or software you cannot spot on a test drive. California keeps a pre-1996 analog, "WARRANTY RETURN," for cars reacquired before the lemon-law brand existed.

Can a lemon-law buyback be a good deal — and what is the catch?

Sometimes. Because a buyback carries no crash or flood damage, it can sell for meaningfully less than a clean-title version of the same car while being mechanically sound — but only if the defect that caused the buyback has actually been identified and fixed. The defect is the entire reason the car exists as a buyback, and it is the one thing you must pin down before you pay. Any blanket "resale drops X percent" figure you see online is an unsourced estimate; treat the discount as real but unquantified.

Do not treat a buyback as a bargain until you can name the specific nonconformity in the repair record and confirm the repairs. In states like California, the maker must prepare a notice identifying each reported nonconformity and the repairs attempted — that document is your starting point. Any remaining factory warranty generally stays with the VIN rather than the owner, but confirm the surviving coverage, and whether your state adds a post-buyback warranty, with the manufacturer and the statute — neither substitutes for confirming the defect was fixed.

Does the seller have to tell me a car is a buyback?

In many states, yes — but the obligation comes from the state lemon statute, not the federal Used Car Rule. The FTC's Rule (16 CFR Part 455) requires a dealer to post and hand over a Buyers Guide stating whether the car is sold "as is" or with a warranty, and that Guide overrides contrary "as is" language in the contract. The FTC page does not mention buybacks or lemon law; it defers to the state attorney general, and there is no federal buyback disclosure box.

California is a concrete example (verified at statute on 2026-11-02). Under Cal. Civ. Code §1793.23, anyone who sells, leases, or transfers a branded vehicle must give the buyer a signed disclosure before the transaction, using the statutory text that the vehicle "WAS REPURCHASED BY ITS MANUFACTURER DUE TO A DEFECT" and that the title "HAS BEEN PERMANENTLY BRANDED WITH THE NOTATION 'LEMON LAW BUYBACK.'" The maker must also affix a decal to the left-door frame. Other states set their own disclosure forms, thresholds, and even the brand's name, so verify the rule in the state where you are buying rather than assuming California's applies.

Can a lemon-law buyback brand be washed or removed?

Treat any claim that the brand can be erased with caution. Because federal law requires states to report title brands to NMVTIS, the brand is recorded against the VIN and is designed to survive a move to another state — the core defense against "title washing." California's statute and DMV manual describe the lemon-law buyback brand as permanent. General claims on commercial sites that some states allow removal after inspection were not verified state by state and should not be relied on. The safe discipline is the same as with any clean-title claim: do not trust the paper alone — pull the VIN's brand history from NMVTIS and the issuing state's DMV and confirm the ownership trail, as you would to verify a used car title is clean before buying.

Which VIN check actually proves a buyback title brand?

The authoritative sources are NMVTIS and the state DMV — nothing else is the legal classifier. NMVTIS is the federal system of record for state-applied title brands; consumers cannot buy a report from the government database directly and must use an Approved NMVTIS Data Provider. Carfax and Experian are listed as commercial-only accessors, so a consumer generally cannot receive an NMVTIS report from them.

Who should use which, in plain terms:

  • To prove the brand: buy an NMVTIS report from an approved consumer provider, and cross-check the physical title (and any decal) with the issuing state DMV. In a state like Florida, you can start with the state's own FLHSMV title lookup.
  • To corroborate the story around the brand: any vehicle-history report can add the ownership chain, accident and odometer records, and sales-listing history. These do not replace NMVTIS, but a period where the manufacturer briefly held the car, or a car that bounced between states and dealers, is exactly the pattern worth surfacing.
  • To identify the defect: go to NHTSA for recalls and to the selling dealer for the manufacturer's repair/nonconformity record.

What a VIN check can and can't tell you about a buyback

A VIN history report, from any provider, is a corroboration tool here, not the legal authority on the brand. Use it to confirm the car was not also crashed, flooded, or odometer-rolled on top of the defect, and to screen for the recall family behind the buyback. Then confirm the brand itself at NMVTIS and the state DMV, and confirm the defect and its fix at NHTSA and the dealer.

What to verify by VIN before you pay for a buyback

  1. Pull the brand from NMVTIS via an approved consumer provider, and match it against the physical title and the state DMV record. This is the only step that proves the brand.
  2. Read the manufacturer's nonconformity notice (where state law requires one, as in California) to name the exact defect and the repairs attempted.
  3. Check the defect in NHTSA's recall database by VIN, and ask the selling dealer to confirm whether the relevant recall or repair was completed — a history report shows presence, not remedy.
  4. Confirm the car was not also damaged: accident/airbag-deployment, flood/junk/salvage auction records, and an odometer/rollback check.
  5. Map the ownership and listing trail: a brief manufacturer-held period, or a car that moved across states and dealers, is a red flag worth explaining before you buy.
  6. Verify the disclosure you were given matches your state's lemon-law requirement, and keep it in writing.

Frequently asked questions

What is a manufacturer buyback vehicle? A car the automaker repurchased from its owner because a warranty defect could not be fixed within the limit a state lemon law allows — usually a set number of repair attempts or cumulative days out of service. It is a defect brand, not a crash brand.

Is a manufacturer buyback the same as a salvage title? No. Salvage means an insurer declared the car a total loss, usually after a crash, flood, theft, or fire. A buyback or lemon-law brand means an unfixable warranty defect, with no implied collision, flood, or theft damage.

Can a lemon-law buyback be a good deal? It can sell well below a clean-title equivalent, but only if you first identify the defect that caused the buyback and confirm it was fixed. Without that, the unresolved defect is the risk you are buying.

Does the seller have to disclose a buyback? Disclosure comes from state lemon statutes, not the federal FTC Used Car Rule, which has no buyback box. Rules vary by state; California, for example, requires a signed written disclosure before the sale.

Will a vehicle history report show a lemon-law buyback? It may surface a reported brand, but no commercial report is the legal classifier. The authoritative records are NMVTIS, via an approved data provider, and the state DMV title. Confirm the brand there.

Does the buyback brand follow the car to another state? Federal law requires states to report title brands to NMVTIS, so the brand is recorded against the VIN across state lines. California's statute and DMV manual describe the brand as permanent.

What repair threshold made it a lemon in the first place? It depends on the state. Lemon laws generally trigger after a set number of repair attempts for the same defect or a set number of cumulative days out of service, but the exact numbers vary and must be checked in the relevant state's statute.
